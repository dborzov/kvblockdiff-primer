VI.9

The napkin for on fire

Half a punnet of 4096 cherries at 640 bytes is 1.38 MB per layer. That is what one on-fire stretch costs before its first delta saves a byte, and it also sits in GPU memory for as long as the stretch burns. This page states the economics as a problem of its own, writes its two budgets, and runs them on the measured curve: on the Pin the heavy point is bound by GPU memory, with every assumed input marked.

One on-fire stretch at the reference-tree point costs 0.525×4096×640 bytes, about 1.38 MB, per layer, and 107 MB per sequence once all 78 layers have set the same stretch on fire. Those bytes cross the link once and then sit in GPU memory for as long as the stretch burns. Everything on this page follows from that number, the measured deltas, and the Pin's rig. The traffic side (fD​, nload​, how many stretches burn) is assumed, and marked.

The subproblem, stated

Strip the napkin down the way Part IV stripped the scenario (IV.2 One cherry at a time), and it is a clean problem of its own.

Why it exists. The project’s goal is one number: more concurrent sequences per GPU than HiSparse, at unchanged outputs (VI.6 What "better than HiSparse" means). HiSparse pays two bills per sequence, link bytes per step and GPU bytes held. kvblockdiff changes both, and every change is a product of a byte count and a pick count. The economics is the arithmetic that adds the products up and says which bill binds.

Its inputs, each a named quantity with a home:

input symbol comes from grade today
delta share and dictionary weight ρ, w a scenario of Part IV, measured by the harness in Part VIII measured on the Bench’s values, several points of the curve
misses per layer per step mk HiSparse’s published miss rates; a hot-cache replay of a pick trace (X.5 Queries and picks: a stand-in indexer, X.6 Replaying the hot cache) the Pin’s trace only; none on the Bench
where misses land: fD​, stretches on fire, loads fD​, nD​, nload​ the same replay, with a shelf policy assumed
the decoder keeps up a condition the decode-speed subproblem (X.7 How fast must the decoder be?) unmeasured
the rig NL​, e, k, B, TPOT, Wlink​, MHBM​ a reference setting (III.11 Two reference settings: the Pin and the Bench) frozen

Its outputs. Link bytes per sequence per step, Tstep​; GPU memory per sequence, Mseq​; the two ceilings and their minimum, N=min(Nlink​,NHBM​); and, one page on, tokens per second and dollars per million tokens (VI.11 From bits to dollars).

What it assumes. Fetches overlap with compute, as HiSparse’s do; the decoder keeps up; a ratio measured on 512-byte cherries carries over to 640-byte latents; one trace of one LLM gives the miss rates. Each is marked where it is used.

The rule. The napkin is arithmetic, and arithmetic adds no trust. A result of the napkin carries the grade of its least-trusted input. Today that is the traffic column, so every “1.1×” on this page is an assumed number multiplied by a measured one, and reads as such.

By the numbersWhat the replay measured for the traffic inputs

Since 2026-11-03 the traffic column has values measured on a stand-in LLM (Qwen 2.5 1.5B) and carried to the Pin (Part XI). Misses: 35 / 19 / 9.7 % at 1, 2 and 4 slots per pick, against HiSparse’s 30 / 13.4 / 6.7 % (XI.5 The hot cache, replayed). Four dictionaries of 4096 positions: fD​ 0.36–0.54 at 0.10–0.13 loads per step, not 0.85 and 0.05 (XI.8 The shelf, replayed). Run with every design at its best hot-cache size, this napkin gives 56 sequences per GPU for four tree dictionaries at the assumed 0.85, 88–108 for the best tree shelf on the measured counts, and 127 for HiSparse with its hot cache resized to about 4740 slots (XI.10 The slot price). The equations and figures on this page keep the assumed inputs.

Takeaway
  • The economics is one subproblem among five: it takes ρ and w from the scenario, the misses and fD​ from a replay, the decoder’s speed from its own test, the rig from a setting.
  • It outputs Tstep​, Mseq​, N, then tokens per second and dollars.
  • A napkin result is only as good as its least-trusted input; today that is the traffic, which is assumed.

Running themeEvery helper charges a tax

A dictionary adds two taxes to HiSparse’s bill: link bytes for loading it (nload​NL​we) and GPU bytes for keeping it (NL​ewres​). The deltas must save more than both, or the dictionary loses.

☞ Things to try
  1. Set fD​ to 1 and ρ to 0.112, the tree’s delta share with every miss a δ-miss. Even the full-basis line tolerates about 0.06 loads per step per layer.
  2. Set ρ to 0.9. Every line starts just under 1, and even rare loads of a codebook push it over.
  3. Raise nD​ to 16 at the default settings. Each codebook must now live about 50 steps: more residents means more loads to pay for.
Takeaway

  • With the Pin’s 274 misses per layer per step, that is about 42 steps at nD​=4, fD​=0.85, or 30 at nD​=1, fD​=0.3 (assumed pairs).
  • Loads must satisfy nload​w<fD​(1−ρ)mk: under 0.096 per step per layer at the tree point, under 0.9 at the light point.

Both are under HiSparse’s 109. One on-fire dictionary is 0.525 of a hot cache per layer, and the hot cache is the thing GPU memory on the Pin can hold only 147 of. On the Pin, heavy on-fire dictionaries are bound by GPU memory, and no reduction of link traffic moves that bound. The link side was never the problem; at nD​=1, fD​=0.3 and nload​=0.01 it allows 135 while memory allows 96.

A dictionary that is half the punnet costs as much GPU memory as half a hot cache, and the hot cache is what GPU memory on the Pin already cannot afford more of.

The way out is the one the all-resident corner pointed at. A reference tree already holds half the punnet’s cherries verbatim, so while a stretch is on fire its hot-cache slots are redundant, and the hot cache only needs room for the misses of stretches on ice, about (1−fD​)B slots. That is lever 2 (VI.6 What "better than HiSparse" means), with the dictionary as the compressed slots:

The 120 assumes nload​=0.01, each dictionary living about 100 steps, three times its break-even; at the break-even rate of one load per 30 steps the link allows 110 and the gain is 1 %. The napkin drops the leaf deltas that would also have to be kept (0.11 of a cherry each on average) and treats (1−fD​)B as enough room for the ice; both are approximations. With one stretch on fire per layer the design edges past HiSparse; with four, the dictionaries alone are more than two hot caches and memory binds again.

The curve is convex, so a lighter point trades saving per miss for a smaller load and a smaller footprint. The same arithmetic at each measured point, with b=4096 and the Pin’s cherries:

At the 10 % point a dictionary is a tenth of a hot cache and pays for itself after about 1 050 δ-misses, but four of them cost 40 % of a hot cache, which leaves memory at about 105 sequences, under HiSparse. One or two on fire per layer, catching 30–50 % of the misses (assumed), give about 120, the 1.1× the tree point reaches only with lever 2, and without a tree decoder. The 2 % point loads almost for free and saves almost nothing per miss; it is the tiny-dictionary corner, where everything can stay resident and fire is not needed.

By the numbersA machine with more GPU memory per link byte

The memory bound scales with MHBM​; the link bound does not. Give the same rig 60 GB for orchards: HiSparse stays link-bound at 109, while one on-fire tree dictionary per layer (fD​=0.3, nload​=0.01) becomes link-bound at about 135, 1.24×. A what-if, since the Pin is frozen; the design pays sooner wherever GPU memory is cheap relative to link bytes.

Running themeThe access pattern decides the form

The reference tree reads about 120 cherries’ worth per cold decode. That read pattern is harmless in GPU memory and ruinous over a link, which is why the dictionary must be resident, and residency is what the memory budget charges for. The access pattern of the decoder fixes which budget binds.

Takeaway
