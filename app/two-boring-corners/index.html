VI.7

The two boring corners

Walk the measured compressibility curve from one end to the other. At the left end the dictionary is so small that it can stay resident everywhere, and the design is solo coding with a per-punnet rule. At the right end it is half the punnet, and keeping it resident for every stretch means keeping half the orchard on the GPU. Both ends are settled. The design lives in between.

Part VIII left this site a measured compressibility curve. Under a dictionary budget of 2 % of the punnet the best compressor reached a ratio of 0.753; at 10 %, 0.710; at 25 %, 0.691; unconstrained, 0.682 at a dictionary of 36 %. On another screen the reference-tree compressor put 52.5 % of the punnet in its dictionary and reached 0.636. The reading of that curve on this page is that its two ends are things everyone already knows how to build, and the design question is the stretch between them.

The tiny-dictionary corner

Start at the left end. A dictionary of 2 % of a 4096-cherry punnet is about 86 cherries’ worth. All twenty-five stretches of a 100K-token sequence on the Pin could keep theirs on the GPU for half a hot cache, and nothing would ever need loading. The campaigns’ light compressor 129 shows what such a dictionary holds: a location and a scale per furrow, four cherry classes and a small table for linear prediction, with no reference cherries (V.3 Furrow statistics: the Anchor). Every cherry is coded on its own against that description, and every miss moves a delta of about two thirds of a raw cherry.

That is solo coding with a per-punnet rule. It is the first thing anyone tries, and the literature has already priced it. Coding 8-bit values one element at a time is worth about 1.1–1.3× (V.2 Solo: the Hermit, IX.4 SplitZip (2026)). The information theory says why there is no more to find there. A compressor that looks at one element alone can do no better than the order-0 entropy of its furrow, which for a bell-shaped E4M3 furrow is 6.47 bits of 8 (V.1 The shape of every compressor, and its bill). The campaigns’ solo ideal over six layers was 6.22 bits, a ratio of 0.778; their light compressor, with its per-punnet locations and classes, reached 0.686 on cherry v[t, l, :, :], or 5.49 bits. The per-punnet rule buys about 0.7 bit on top of solo. Everything a punnet’s cherries share beyond their common distribution stays on the table, and VIII.14 What the half says, and what it does not measured that remainder at about a fifth of the bits.

CarefulAn entropy-coded number

The 0.686 is an rANS stream. The miss path wants fixed-length deltas, and at this depth the fixed-length rate runs about a bit above the entropy rate (V.11 Two rates: entropy and fixed-length). The corner is less attractive on the wire than on the ledger.

Boring does not mean useless. All-resident light dictionaries cost no loads, no counter and no residency question, and the calculator of VI.10 A what-if calculator shows what they buy: at ρ=0.665 with 86 cherries’ worth per stretch resident everywhere, about 96 sequences per GPU against HiSparse’s 109, 0.88×, because even half a hot cache of dictionaries per layer costs more memory than the cheaper misses give back on the Pin.

Takeaway
  • A dictionary of 2 % is a description of the punnet’s distribution; coding against it is solo coding with a per-punnet rule.
  • The literature prices per-cherry coding at 1.1–1.3×, bounded by the order-0 entropy (6.47 bits for E4M3); the campaigns’ light compressor reached 0.686.
  • The corner is already published and holds no design question; on the Pin, all resident, it is 0.88× HiSparse.

The all-resident corner

Now the right end. The reference-tree compressor’s dictionary is half the punnet, made of the punnet’s own cherries laid along a reference tree (V.6 Siblings and reference trees). A cold decode of one cherry walks its path to the root and reads about 120 cherries’ worth (VIII.9 Reading one cherry), so the dictionary has to be in GPU memory before a miss can be served as a delta. The simplest way to arrange that is to keep every stretch’s dictionary resident for the whole sequence.

Count what that costs on the Pin. One dictionary is 0.525×4096≈2150 cherries’ worth per layer; a 100K-token sequence has 25 stretches, so about 53 800 cherries’ worth per layer, about thirteen hot caches of 4096 slots, or 2.7 GB of dictionaries per sequence over 78 layers before the hot cache itself. The result is about 11 sequences per GPU against HiSparse’s 109, with GPU memory binding and the link idle (it could feed about 436, since every miss is a ninth of a cherry).

The corner reduces to a sentence. Keep half of every orchard in GPU memory, compressed. That is a respectable design, this site’s lever 2 with the victim tier as its gentle first form (VI.12 A victim tier of deltas). It is also the thing III.6 Everything must be kept says a GPU cannot afford and III.7 HiSparse: a library with a reading desk exists to avoid. HiSparse moved the orchard to host memory so that the GPU could hold a small desk per sequence; filling the GPU back up with dictionaries undoes the move.

By the numbersThe same corner for lighter dictionaries

All-resident fails well before the tree point. At 10 % of the punnet, twenty-five dictionaries of 410 cherries’ worth are two and a half hot caches per layer, about 42 sequences per GPU at 4096 slots. Even the 2 % dictionaries, all resident, cost half a hot cache and leave the GPU at 0.88× HiSparse. On the Pin, “resident for every stretch” is affordable only for dictionaries of a few cherries’ worth, the furrow statistics of V.3 Furrow statistics: the Anchor.

Takeaway

  • The corner is “keep half the orchard in HBM”, the memory wall that HiSparse was built to get around.

The range between

Between the two ends the curve is convex. Two per cent of dictionary buys 42 % of the gain the unconstrained compressor reaches on that screen, ten per cent buys 77 %, a quarter buys 93 %. Each step to the right buys less delta for more dictionary. The dictionaries that carry most of the gain start at about a tenth of the punnet, and those are already too large to keep for every stretch. They have to live in host memory, like the orchard itself, and come to the GPU only when their stretch is wanted.

the interesting rangethis project's own words
  • What it is. The dictionaries that are large enough to carry a punnet’s shared structure and too large to keep resident for every stretch. They sit in host memory and are brought to the GPU for the stretches the indexer keeps wanting.
  • Where it is on the curve. From about a tenth of the punnet (410 cherries’ worth at b=4096) up to the tree point (half the punnet). The measured points at 10 %, 25 %, 36 % and 52.5 % all lie in it.
  • Easy to confuse with the dictionary shelf, the mechanism that serves this range; and with the old draft’s “heavy” dictionaries of 256 cherries’ worth. Here a dictionary is heavy when it is a sizeable share of its punnet.

This is the site’s own bet, with sizes filled in. VI.3 The shelf, and two kinds of miss added a shelf of nD​ dictionaries next to the hot cache; VI.4 The same trace, with dictionaries showed that the shelf pays only if misses crowd into a few stretches that stay busy. The old draft priced it with assumed weights of 32 and 256 cherries’ worth. The curve replaces those with 410 to 2 150, and with the saving each one buys per miss.

☞ Things to try
  1. Compare the drop from the 2 % point to the 10 % point with the drop from 36 % to 52.5 %. The first lowers the delta share by 0.12 for 0.08 of dictionary share; the second, across two screens, lowers it by 0.21 for 0.17, and most of that is bytes moved from one share to the other, since a tree’s dictionary is made of cherries (VIII.14 What the half says, and what it does not).
  2. Follow the solo line to the baseline. Every dot below it has paid for its dictionary on the ledger. None of them has yet paid for loading it, which is the next two pages.

The interesting range is where a dictionary is worth loading and too heavy to leave loaded.

Two facts must hold for the range to pay. The bytes are measured now; the curve says what each dictionary saves per miss. The traffic is not; whether misses crowd into few stretches for long enough to amortize a load is the heavy-stretch property, never replayed (VII.8 Lucky Louis, stretch by stretch, X.6 Replaying the hot cache). VI.8 On fire and ice: a shelf at stretch level designs the mechanism; VI.9 The napkin for on fire prices it with measured bytes and assumed traffic.

Running themeWhich picks, versus what is inside them

The curve is a “what” measurement. It says how alike the cherries of a punnet are, in bits. Whether the indexer keeps returning to the same stretch is a “which” question, and the interesting range stands or falls on it.
