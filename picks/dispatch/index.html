XIPart XI · The Bachelor: picks and misses, measured
XI.1

The result on one page

Part VI estimated what kvblockdiff gains with a traffic column it had to assume. On 3 November 2026 the plan’s stand-in indexer and its hot-cache replay ran, on a smaller LLM than the Bench, and filled that column with measured counts. The stand-in reproduces HiSparse’s miss rates within 1.5×. Misses pile into stretches, but less as the context grows. With the measured counts, the design that serves the most sequences per GPU keeps no dictionary on the GPU at all.

VI.9 The napkin for on fire ended Part VI with two budgets per sequence, link bytes and GPU bytes, and a table of inputs. The bytes column was measured: the campaigns of Part VIII had given ρ and w at several points of the curve. The traffic column was not. How many picks miss, how many of those misses find their stretch’s dictionary on the shelf, and how often a dictionary must be loaded were HiSparse’s numbers or guesses: 274 misses per layer per step, fD​ = 0.85, nload​ = 0.05. The plan proposed to measure them with a stand-in indexer and a replay (X.5 Queries and picks: a stand-in indexer, X.6 Replaying the hot cache). On 3 November both ran. This Part reports what they found.

How this Part runs

Every object on these pages has been met before. The orchard is the store of keys and values (III.3 The orchard). The indexer names k picks per layer per step (III.5 Sparse attention and the indexer). HiSparse keeps recent picks in its hot cache and fetches the rest over the link (III.7 HiSparse: a library with a reading desk); the Lucky Louis property is why that works (III.9 The Lucky Louis property). kvblockdiff adds a dictionary shelf, so that a miss whose stretch is on fire travels as a delta (VI.3 The shelf, and two kinds of miss, VI.8 On fire and ice: a shelf at stretch level). What is new here is the data and the chain of steps that turns it into the napkin’s missing column.

In words:

  1. A dump with queries. The Bench kept keys and values but no queries, so no indexer could run on it. A smaller dump, XI.2 The Bachelor: a dump with queries, kept them.
  2. A stand-in indexer and its trace. The LLM’s own attention, ranked, names the picks of every layer at every step. XI.3 A trace, up close shows 600 real steps of one layer, with every miss and every shelf change.
  3. The replay. HiSparse’s LRU runs over the trace and counts misses (XI.5 The hot cache, replayed); a shelf runs beside it and counts δ-misses and loads (XI.7 Where misses land, XI.8 The shelf, replayed). No byte of the orchard is opened: this is the trace replay of the plan.
  4. Carrying the counts. The Bachelor’s sequences are 27K tokens long and the Pin’s is 100K. Two rules measured inside the dump carry the counts across (XI.4 Carrying numbers from a small LLM, XI.6 The share carries, not the budget, XI.8 › The rule that overturned “the stretch length is absolute”).
  5. The bytes side and the napkin. Only at the end do bytes enter: the campaigns’ MDL ratios, and their split between dictionary and deltas (XI.9 The other half of the bill). The napkin then compares every design at equal GPU memory (XI.10 The slot price, XI.11 The ceiling of the miss path).

A worked example of what the replay counts, from the trace of XI.3 A trace, up close. At every step of layer 9 of sequence 2 the stand-in names 512 positions. Over the 600 steps shown there, 98 of the 512 miss the hot cache on average (19 %), and 58 % of those misses find their stretch’s dictionary on a shelf of four. At the step just after the assistant starts writing, 220 miss, and 190 of them are δ-misses.

Takeaway
  • The chain: a dump with queries, a stand-in indexer, a pick trace, a replay of the hot cache and the shelf, rules that carry the counts to the Pin, then the napkin with the campaigns’ bytes.
  • Everything before the napkin counts which picks are wanted; no byte of the orchard is read.

The result in five readings

  1. The stand-in indexer is calibrated. At the Pin’s share of the context (512 picks of about 25K positions, 2 %), the hot cache misses 35 / 19 / 9.7 % with 1, 2 and 4 slots per pick, against HiSparse’s 30 / 13.4 / 6.7 % on GLM-5.1. That is 1.2–1.5× higher, inside the 2× rule registered in advance, with the same slope (1.07 against 1.08) and the same gap to a clairvoyant policy (1.58 against 1.63) (XI.5 The hot cache, replayed).
  2. The share of the context picked carries across contexts; the count of picks does not. Miss rates for 64 to 2048 picks per step fall on one curve in the share of the context picked. Numbers move to another LLM’s setting at equal share and equal slots per pick (XI.6 The share carries, not the budget).
  3. Misses pile into stretches, and the pile spreads as the context grows. A step’s heaviest stretch holds 3.3 times its shuffled twin’s share of the misses; a shelf catches 3–5 times what the twin catches at equal coverage. At the Pin’s 100K, extrapolated, a shelf of 4 dictionaries of 4096 positions catches 36–54 % of the misses, not the 85 % Part VI assumed, at 0.10–0.13 loads per step (XI.7 Where misses land, XI.8 The shelf, replayed).
  4. A dictionary on the GPU takes memory that could hold hot-cache slots. In the MDL ratio a dictionary is spread over its punnet; in serving it sits in GPU memory whether or not its stretch is fetched. It must save more link bytes than the slots its memory would hold. Tree dictionaries (the campaigns’ best ratio) never do; light ones barely do (XI.9 The other half of the bill, XI.10 The slot price).
  5. The best design measured keeps no dictionary per sequence. At the Pin, each design at its best hot-cache size: HiSparse as published 109.5 sequences per GPU; HiSparse with a resized hot cache 127; light dictionaries on a shelf 127–133; tree dictionaries on a shelf 88–108; a solo code for every miss, with no bytes per sequence, 141 (XI.10 › The napkin at equal GPU memory).

At the Pin, four dictionaries of 4096 positions catch 36–54 % of the misses, not 85 %, and a code with no dictionary serves more sequences than any shelf.

Takeaway
  • Stand-in calibrated: 35 / 19 / 9.7 % against HiSparse’s 30 / 13.4 / 6.7 %.
  • Miss rates follow the share of the context picked; a shelf’s catch follows the stretch length over the context to the power 1.27.
  • At the Pin: 4 × 4096 catches 36–54 % of misses; a solo code on every miss serves 141 sequences per GPU against 127 for HiSparse resized.

The potential of each approach, at a glance

Sequences per GPU at the Pin, each design at the hot-cache size that suits it best, next to what limits it. The two levers of VI.6 What "better than HiSparse" means are both in play: a design wins by shrinking misses (the link bound) or by freeing GPU memory (the memory bound).

Takeaway
  • Resized HiSparse 127 is the baseline; light shelf 127–133; solo on every miss 141; tree shelf 88–108.
  • The ceiling of the miss path at the Pin is 166 sequences per GPU, +31 %, at the whole-sequence floor of 0.57.

How to read this Part

The pages follow the chain of the diagram above: the setting (XI.2–4), the hot cache (XI.5–6), stretches and the shelf (XI.7–8), the bytes and the napkin (XI.9–11), the indexer (XI.12) and the consequences (XI.13).

Every number on these pages has one of four grades, and says which.

  1. Measured: an exact replay of the stored traces, every step from position 8192 on counted. Means over 140 (sequence, layer) pairs, with the range of the five per-sequence means in brackets.
  2. Derived: arithmetic or a fit on measured numbers, with the formula.
  3. Extrapolated: carried to the Pin’s 100K context by a rule fitted inside the dump.
  4. Their measurement: HiSparse’s numbers on its one GLM-5.1 trace.

The campaigns’ MDL ratios keep their own grades (verified, code-length estimate, extrapolation; VIII.1 A dispatch from the harness). The caveats that travel with every number are listed on XI.13 › Caveats that travel with every number: a stand-in indexer, one 1.5B LLM, contexts of 24.5K–30K tokens, prefill queries, squeezed scores for V4’s rows, the campaigns’ ratios carried to the Pin’s latents, one genre of text.
