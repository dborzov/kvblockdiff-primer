The toy of [[#toolbox-the-bill]] already ran a codebook: two prototype cherries in the dictionary, one bit in every delta to say which, and residuals that were nearly all zeros for cherries 1 and 4. The bill was 0.25 bit per element of tax and 2.67 bits of dictionary in a punnet of six. The first draft of this project wrote that recipe in one line, "VQ, then Gaussian entropy-encode the error", and it hides a tax that decides everything.

## The Hive as a dictionary {#dictionary}

::: aside reminder Centroids, codebooks and the index tax (II.17, II.18)
[[k-means]] places $\vC$ [[centroid|centroids]] in the middle of the data's clumps; the list is the [[codebook]]. Losslessly, the chosen centroid is a prediction and the exact residual is coded too; naming the centroid costs $\log_2 \vC$ bits, the [[index-tax]]. [[product-quantization]] cuts each vector into $\vnsub$ pieces with a codebook each.
:::

```python the VQ stage: a codebook in the dictionary, an index in every delta
def fit(punnet, C):
    return kmeans(punnet.astype(np.float32), C).round().astype(np.int8)   # (C, d): C prototype cherries

def encode(cherry, codebook):
    idx = np.argmin(((codebook.astype(np.int16) - cherry) ** 2).sum(axis=1))  # nearest prototype
    residual = cherry.astype(np.int16) - codebook[idx]                         # exact
    return idx, residual                      # the index is the per-cherry tax: log2(C) bits

def decode(idx, residual, codebook):
    return (codebook[idx] + residual).astype(np.int8)
```

- **Dictionary.** $\vC$ centroids, each a full cherry: $\vw = \vC$. Product quantization with $\vnsub$ codebooks of 256 pieces each is also 256 cherries' worth in total.
- **Per-cherry tax.** The index, $\log_2 \vC$ bits per cherry; product quantization pays $8\,\vnsub$ bits per cherry.

For the Bench's 512-element cherry the two lines come to:

| codebook | tax per cherry | tax per element | codebook at $\vb = 4096$ | at $\vb = 65\,536$ |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $\vC = 16$ | 4 bits | 0.008 | 0.03 | 0.002 |
| $\vC = 256$ | 8 bits | 0.016 | 0.5 | 0.03 |
| $\vC = 4096$ | 12 bits | 0.023 | 8 | 0.5 |

The index is cheap on long cherries. The codebook is not: 256 centroids need punnets of tens of thousands of cherries before their line fades, which makes a codebook a sequence-scope dictionary rather than a stretch-scope one.

::: takeaway
- VQ stores $\vC$ centroids ($\vw = \vC$) and pays $\log_2 \vC$ bits per cherry to name one.
- On long cherries the index is cheap; the codebook is the expensive line unless many cherries share it.
:::

## The bound: no win over a free rotation on bell-shaped data {#bound}

Think of the index as a message of $\log_2 \vC$ bits about the cherry. A message of $R$ bits can tell the receiver at most $R$ bits it did not know. For bell-shaped data, [[rate-distortion]] theory makes this exact: beyond what a rotation captures for free, an index of $\log_2 \vC$ bits shrinks the residual's entropy by **at most** $\log_2 \vC$ bits per cherry, which is what the index costs.

$$
\vgain_{\text{VQ}} \;\le\; \underbrace{\vgain_{\text{KLT}}}_{\text{a rotation, free per cherry}} + \underbrace{\frac{\log_2 \vC}{\vd}}_{\text{= the index tax}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\text{net}_{\text{VQ}} \;\le\; \vgain_{\text{KLT}} - \underbrace{\frac{8\,\vC}{\vb}}_{\text{codebook}}
$$ {#eq-toolbox-vq-bound}

Equality needs an ideal quantiser; k-means is worse. Product quantization does not escape, because the bound adds up over the pieces. The only way out is data that is **not** bell-shaped: tight clusters, where knowing the cluster says far more than any rotation can. A seeded Monte Carlo checks both halves.

Dashed: the KLT's gain, $\tfrac12\log_2(\mathrm{AM}/\mathrm{GM})$, with no tax and its basis not charged. **Switch** between two synthetic punnets of $\vb = 16\,384$ vectors of $\vd = 64$: correlated bell-shaped data (a rank-8 signal of variance 16 per direction plus unit noise) and clustered data (32 cluster centres plus unit noise). `analysis/homework_a_vq.py`.

::: try
1. On the correlated data, compare the best net bar (+0.22 at $\vC = 64$) with the dashed line (+0.50): VQ gets less than half of what the free rotation gets.
2. Look at $\vC = 4096$: the gross saving keeps growing, but the codebook alone costs 2 bits per element, and 4096 centroids fit on 16 384 vectors memorise noise.
3. Switch to clustered data. At $\vC = 64$, two centroids per real cluster, VQ nets +1.45 against the rotation's +0.68.
:::

::: theme every-tax
The tax has two parts, and both are visible as bars. The index is paid by every cherry, the codebook by every punnet. On bell-shaped data the gross saving can never outrun them by more than a free rotation gets anyway; on clustered data it can, by a lot.
:::

::: takeaway
- On Gaussian data VQ's gain is at most the KLT's plus its own index tax: a taxed copy of the rotation.
- It nets a clear win only on genuinely clustered data; whether cherries cluster is a measurement.
:::

## What the campaigns found {#measured}

The measurement came in, and it answered the napkin's open question in an unexpected way ([[#measured-codebooks-and-rotations]]). Built as real compressors on the layer screen (`V · all layers · cherry (2, 256) · stretch 4096 · leash 293`), each stacked on linear prediction with no reference tree: RQ, a codebook followed by a second one for what the first leaves, saved **0.0029** of ratio on every one of 14 pieces of the screen, verified; product quantization 0.0017 (a quick trial); both stayed about 0.05 behind a compressor with a reference tree on the same screen. Stacked on tree *and* prediction, offered codebooks of 4, 16 or 64 words or none and told to pick whatever cost the fewest bits, the encoder chose **no codebook on 72 of 72 punnets**. The first campaign had seen the same thing earlier in an estimate: invented average cherries lost to real ones, 0.780 against 0.753 on eleven punnets. The reason fits in a sentence. A codeword is stored only to help others, so its $\ve$ bytes are pure tax; a [[#toolbox-reference-cherries|reference cherry]] must be stored anyway, so using it costs a pointer of about 12 bits and nothing more, and when the text repeats a real earlier copy is a near twin where a centroid is only an average. Clusters do exist, in a minority of cherries: on one layer-3 punnet, 42 % of RQ's saving came from the top tenth of cherries. OPQ, binary codes and LSH acted on no cluster at all. No codebook was tried at 16 384 tokens or on `(6, 2, 256)` cherries, where it would be cheaper per cherry, so the branch is thin rather than closed.

::: aside source Receipts
RQ on prediction alone: r26-t0011 (0.69421 against the control r26-t0002 at 0.69716, isolated, 14/14 segments); PQ: r26-t0008 (quick); no codebook chosen on tree + prediction: r26-t0010 (quick); cluster share and delta sizes: r26 iter-1-3. Centroids vs real entries 0.780 vs 0.753: C1 `research/c1/KNOWLEDGE.md` [sim/tree]. Coverage: r25 iter-1-1. PART-XI-SPEC §1.7.

::: takeaway
- Real codebooks gained at most 0.0029 on prediction alone and were never chosen once a reference tree was present.
- A codeword is stored only to help others; a reference cherry is stored anyway. The bound held, and the reason has a name.
:::
