Pick one [[entry]] from the middle of a long conversation: token 50,000, layer 20. Now give the coder a second entry for free, the one at token 49,999, and ask it to store the first one exactly. How many bits per element does the free neighbour save? What if the free entry sits at token 40,000 instead? Or at token 12?

Nobody knows. Not roughly, not at all: nobody has published this number for 8-bit KV, in bits. That gap is the first and most important question of the project.

## The question, in one sentence {#question}

::: keycard Q1 in one line
$$
\frac{1}{\vd}\Big[\underbrace{\vH(\vE_{\vt}) - \vH(\vE_{\vt} \mid \vE_{\vt - \vgap})}_{\text{bits a neighbour } \vgap \text{ tokens back saves}}\Big] \quad \text{for } \vgap = 1, 2, 4, \dots
$$
How many bits per element does a neighbour at token distance $\vgap$ save, and how does that change as $\vgap$ grows?
:::

::: aside reminder Fallen-apple and upstairs similarity
[[fallen-apple|Fallen-apple similarity]] is the hypothesis that an entry is cheaper to store exactly given an entry of the same layer a small token distance $\vgap$ away, and the closer the cheaper — a curve over $\vgap$, not a threshold. [[upstairs|Upstairs similarity]] is the same idea one layer down. Both are members of [[neighbour]] similarity ([[#idea-neighbour-similarity]]).
:::

The difference of two entropies above is the [[mutual-information]] between an entry and its neighbour. Written out in full, Q1 has four parts.

1. **Measure and describe it.** What does the curve of bits saved against $\vgap$ look like? And the same with the neighbour one floor down (upstairs)?
2. **Size.** Is it big enough that algorithms built on it are a good idea at all? The exchange rate is harsh: one bit per element needs 75 % of the remaining variance explained ([[#school-exchange-rate]]).
3. **Where.** Keys and values separately. At 8-bit precision, because in 16-bit formats the lowest bits are close to noise and cap every lossless gain ([[#missed-mantissa-and-grid]]). Layer by layer. In more than one model family.
4. **Against what.** Every gain is counted against [[solo]] coding, which already saves ~1.1–1.3× with no neighbour at all.

It comes first because without it nothing else matters: if the curve is flat at zero, a [[dictionary]] has nothing to remember. And nothing we already know answers it.

- **HiSparse's hit rate** is evidence about the *queries*: the [[indexer]] re-selects the same positions from step to step. That is the [[lucky-louis]], and a toy with keys that cannot be compressed by a single bit reproduces it ([[#idea-the-leak]]).
- **Lossy papers** (2-bit keys, quantization studies) say what the model *tolerates*. They do not say what is *predictable*.
- **The direction** is a theorem: knowing a neighbour never raises the entropy on average. Only the size is open, and size is everything here.

::: theme bits-unit
Q1 is asked in bits on purpose, not in cosine similarity or perplexity. Bits per element times the entry size is bytes on the link, so the answer converts straight into the units of the two levers.
:::

::: takeaway
- Q1: how many bits does a neighbour at distance $\vgap$ save, and how does it fade with $\vgap$ — for keys and values, at 8 bits, per layer?
- It is the existence question: everything else assumes a usable answer.
- HiSparse's hit rate and lossy results do not answer it; they are about other things.
:::

## What yes, no and partly would look like {#outcomes}

Before any data, here is what each answer would look like, and what it would make us do.

| answer | what the distance curve looks like | what we would do |
|---|---|---|
| **yes, local** | high at small $\vgap$, fading with $\vgap$, clearly above the shuffled curve up to $\vgap \approx$ 1K | block dictionaries; read the block size $\vb$ off the fading length; go on to Q2 and Q3 at [[scope-block]] |
| **partly: per request** | flat, but positive at every $\vgap$ | one dictionary per request ([[scope-request]]); Q3 stops mattering; a simpler system, still new |
| **partly: keys only** | keys fade with $\vgap$; values sit on the solo rate | plan for values capping the gain: half of every entry's bytes stay at the solo rate, whatever the keys do |
| **no** | flat at zero, on top of the shuffled curve | publish the negative with its controls; turn to a global fixed-length coder for the miss path (solo coding, ~1.1–1.3×, no dictionaries) |

The first experiment of the plan turns this table into a rule with numbers. It reads two [[scope-gain|scope gains]], each the best a cheap dictionary type manages, keys and values weighted by their bytes, on the better of the two 8-bit formats.

::: aside reminder Scope gains
$\vgainreq$ is the bits per element a per-request dictionary saves over a global one; $\vgainblk$ is what per-block dictionaries save on top of that ([[#homework-scope]]). Both are measured at fixed dictionary type.
:::

::: figure fig-questions-decision qDecision
**The wave-1 decision rule as a map** (schematic: thresholds only, no data). Horizontal axis: $\vgainreq$, the gain of a per-request dictionary over a global one; vertical axis: $\vgainblk$, the extra gain of per-block dictionaries; both in bits per element. The shaded regions are the rule of [[#plan-decision-rule]]: total below 0.25 bit — no usable structure; 0.25–0.75 — marginal; at least 0.75 with $\vgainblk \ge 0.4$ — block scale; at least 0.75 with $\vgainblk < 0.15$ — request scale. The grey band is a case the rule as first written does not cover. Drag the knob (or use the sliders or arrow keys) to a possible measurement; the readout gives the reading, the next step, and the share of variance the gains would require by the exchange rate $\tfrac12\log_2\frac{1}{1-\vRsq}$.
:::

::: try
1. Put the knob at $\vgainreq = 0.3$, $\vgainblk = 0.55$. The verdict is "block scale". Now read the last line: the two gains together need about 69 % of the variance a global dictionary leaves behind to be explained. That is a lot.
2. Slide $\vgainblk$ down to 0.25 while keeping $\vgainreq$ at 0.8. You land in the grey band, which the first version of the rule forgot. We read it as marginal.
3. Find the smallest total that still escapes "none". A quarter of a bit already needs about 29 % of the variance explained — modest, but not nothing.
:::

The thresholds are judgement calls, not theorems; their value is that they are written down *before* the data.

::: takeaway
- Yes (fading curve), per-request (flat positive), keys-only and no (flat zero) each lead to a different design or result.
- The decision rule reads two scope gains: 0.75 bit in total and 0.4 from blocks is a clear yes; under 0.25 is a no.
- The rule had a gap (total ≥ 0.75 with a middling block gain); we treat it as marginal.
:::

## Where Q1 plugs in {#links}

Q1 is the hinge between the properties of Part IV and everything after them.

- **Properties.** It asks about the two members of neighbour similarity: [[fallen-apple]] along tokens, [[upstairs]] along layers. It deliberately does *not* ask about the Lucky Louis property, which is about which entries are wanted ([[#idea-the-leak]]).
- **Levers.** A δ-miss moves $\vrho\,\ve$ bytes instead of $\ve$ ([[#idea-two-levers]]). The delta ratio $\vrho$ is bits per element over 8, and those bits are what Q1 measures. [[lever|Lever 2]], deltas inside the hot cache, needs a small $\vrho$ too.
- **Frameworks.** [[#questions-framework-a]] measures Q1 directly, cheaply, in about an hour of compute. [[#questions-framework-b]] measures it in system units: the gap between the best block coder and solo coding is fallen-apple similarity in its operational form.
- **Plan.** Experiments [[#plan-experiment-1a|1a]] and [[#plan-experiment-1b|1b]] produce the curve and the scope gains, with the [[shuffle-control]] that certifies them.

A flat answer would still be a result: nobody has a shuffle-controlled curve of bits against distance at 8 bits, and a flat one would tell everyone building KV coders to stop looking for neighbour structure ([[#missed-a-measurement-is-a-result]]).


::: takeaway
- Q1 measures the bytes-side properties (fallen-apple, upstairs), never the which-side one (Lucky Louis).
- It sets the floor of $\vrho$, and with it the payoff of both levers.
- Frameworks A and B answer it in wave 1; a flat answer is publishable.
:::
