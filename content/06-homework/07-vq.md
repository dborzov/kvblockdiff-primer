The first draft of this project said, in passing: *"VQ, then Gaussian entropy-encode the error."* It is a natural thought. [[vq]] finds, for each entry, the nearest of $\vC$ prototype vectors; if entries look like a few prototypes, the error should be small and cheap.

The first draft's phrase has a proper name, lossless [[predictive-coding]] with a [[centroid]] as the prediction. And it hides a tax that turns out to decide everything.

## The Hive as a dictionary {#dictionary}

::: aside reminder Centroids, codebooks and the index tax (II.17, II.18)
- [[k-means]] places $\vC$ [[centroid|centroids]] in the middle of the data's clumps; the list of centroids is the [[codebook]] ([[#school-clusters]]).
- Losslessly, the chosen centroid is a prediction; the exact residual is stored too. Naming the centroid costs $\log_2 \vC$ bits: the [[index-tax]].
- [[product-quantization]] cuts each vector into $\vnsub$ pieces with a codebook each ([[#school-vq-vs-klt]]).
:::

- **What it stores:** $\vC$ centroids, each a full entry. Weight $\vw = \vC$. (Product quantization: $\vnsub$ codebooks of 256 pieces each, also 256 entries' worth in total.)
- **Per-entry tax:** the index, $\log_2 \vC$ bits per entry; product quantization pays $8\vnsub$ bits per entry.
- **Prediction:** the nearest centroid. Residual: the entry minus it, exactly.

Here is what the two costs come to for $\vd = 640$ elements per entry:

| codebook | tax per entry | tax per element | codebook at $\vb = 4096$ | codebook at $\vb = 65536$ |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $\vC = 16$ | 4 bits | 0.006 | 0.03 | 0.002 |
| $\vC = 256$ | 8 bits | 0.013 | 0.5 | 0.03 |
| $\vC = 4096$ | 12 bits | 0.019 | 8 | 0.5 |

(Per element, in bits: the tax is $\log_2 \vC/\vd$; the codebook is $8\vC/\vb$.)

The index is cheap on 640-element entries. The codebook is not: a 256-centroid codebook needs blocks of tens of thousands of entries before it fades, which makes it a per-request dictionary rather than a per-block one ([[#homework-amortization-rule]]).

::: takeaway
- VQ stores $\vC$ centroids ($\vw = \vC$) and pays $\log_2 \vC$ bits per entry to name one.
- On long entries the index is cheap; the codebook is the expensive part unless many entries share it.
:::

## The bound: no win over a free rotation on bell-shaped data {#bound}

How much can naming a centroid save? Think of the index as a message of $\log_2 \vC$ bits about the entry. A message of $R$ bits can tell the receiver at most $R$ bits it did not know. For bell-shaped ([[gaussian]]) data, [[rate-distortion]] theory makes this precise: beyond what a linear rotation already captures for free, an index of $\log_2 \vC$ bits shrinks the residual's entropy by **at most** $\log_2 \vC$ bits per entry — just what the index costs.

$$
\vgain_{\text{VQ}} \;\le\; \underbrace{\vgain_{\text{KLT}}}_{\text{what a rotation gets free}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\log_2 \vC}{\vd}}_{\text{= the index tax}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\text{net}_{\text{VQ}} \;\le\; \vgain_{\text{KLT}} - \frac{8\,\vC}{\vb}
$$ {#eq-hwa-vq-bound}

Read it in words:

1. On correlated bell-shaped data, VQ's centroids do settle in the directions where the data spreads. So VQ beats *no prediction* — but it is recovering the rotation's gain, and paying an index for it.
2. Equality needs an ideal quantizer. k-means is worse, so real VQ stays below the bound.
3. Product quantization does not escape: the bound adds up over the pieces.
4. The only way out is data that is **not** bell-shaped: tight clusters, where knowing the cluster tells you far more than any rotation can.

::: takeaway
- On Gaussian data, VQ's gain is at most the KLT's plus its own index tax: it cannot beat the untaxed rotation.
- It nets a clear win only on genuinely clustered data.
:::

## The Monte Carlo check {#check}

The VQ row contradicts the instinct the project started from, so we checked it numerically, as the source draft did. k-means codebooks of $\vC$ centroids were fit on one block of $\vb = 16{,}384$ vectors in $\vd = 64$ dimensions, and every cost was charged: the index, $\log_2 \vC/64$ bits per element, and the codebook, $8\vC/\vb$.

::: figure fig-hwa-vq hwaVqCheck
**Can a codebook win losslessly?** For each codebook size $\vC$ (horizontal axis), three bars in bits per element: the **gross** saving of the VQ residual over coding each channel alone (measured: per-channel [[histogram]] entropies of exact integer codes), the **tax** (index plus codebook), and the **net** = gross − tax. Dashed line: the KLT's gain, $\tfrac12\log_2(\mathrm{AM}/\mathrm{GM})$, with no tax and its basis not charged. **Switch** between the two synthetic data sets: correlated bell-shaped data (a rank-8 signal of variance 16 per direction plus unit noise) and clustered data (32 cluster centres plus unit noise). Seeded Monte Carlo, `analysis/homework_a_vq.py`.
:::

::: try
1. On the correlated data, compare the best net bar with the dashed line. VQ gets less than half of what the free rotation gets.
2. Look at $\vC = 4096$: the gross saving keeps growing, but the codebook alone costs 2 bits per element.
3. Switch to clustered data. At $\vC = 64$, two centroids per real cluster, VQ nets about twice the rotation's gain.
:::

Our numbers, next to the source draft's:

| data | VQ net, best | at $\vC$ | KLT, no tax | source draft |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|:---|
| correlated, bell-shaped | +0.22 | 64 | +0.50 | VQ ≤ +0.25, KLT +0.50 |
| 32 clusters | +1.45 | 64 | +0.68 | VQ +1.53, KLT +0.68 |

They agree to a few hundredths. Two things the source draft did not say:

- **The bound looks broken at $\vC = 4096$, and is not.** The gross saving there (0.86) exceeds KLT + index (0.69), because 4096 centroids fit on 16,384 vectors memorize part of the noise — four vectors per centroid. The 2-bit codebook bill is the price of that memory, and the net is about −1.3.
- **The KLT line is slightly flattering.** Its own $64 \times 64$ basis in BF16, charged to this block, would cost 0.06 bit per element.

::: theme every-tax
Here the tax has two parts, and both are visible as bars. The index is paid by every entry, the codebook by every block. On bell-shaped data the gross saving can never outrun them by more than a free rotation gets anyway; on clustered data it can, by a lot.
:::

Do KV entries cluster — by token type, by position in a prompt template, by role? Nobody has measured it at 8 bits. The harness answers it directly by comparing the k-means residual with the Gaussian bound ([[#plan-wave1-outputs]]). If they do, the Hive is the dictionary to beat. If not, it is a taxed copy of the Compass.

::: takeaway
- Monte Carlo: on correlated bell-shaped data VQ nets at most +0.22 bit against the rotation's +0.50; on clustered data +1.45 against +0.68.
- Big codebooks memorize noise and pay for it in codebook bytes.
- Whether KV clusters is an open measurement; until then, "VQ, then code the error" is not a plan.
:::
