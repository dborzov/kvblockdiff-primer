II.12

The exchange rate: variance explained → bits

A predictor that explains three quarters of the variance saves one bit per value; two bits need 94 %. This page derives that exchange rate from the last page’s rule — and shows why it is harsh.

“My predictor is 90 % right.” It sounds like a triumph. In bits, if “90 % right” means it explains 90 % of the variance, it saves 1.66 bits per value. A predictor that explains half the variance — still a respectable model by most standards — saves half a bit.

This page derives that exchange rate in three lines, and explains why it is so stingy.

Variance explained

Any predictor splits the variance of the values into two parts: the part it explains and the part left in the residual.

R²statistics

  • What it is: the share of a value’s variance that a predictor accounts for, R2, between 0 and 1. The residual keeps the rest:

    R2=1−variance of the valuevariance of the residual​,variance of the residual=(1−R2)σ2(II.12·1)

    standard deviation [same as the value] — Typical distance from the mean; σ2 is the variance. II.11

  • Example: the previous-value predictor on the Gull Rock temperatures leaves residuals with σ=3.05 tenths, against 24.2 tenths for the readings themselves. R2=1−(3.05/24.2)2≈0.984: 98.4 % of the variance explained.

  • Easy to confuse with: the share of values guessed exactly right. A predictor can explain 98 % of the variance and still be off on almost every reading — by a little.

ReminderVariance and standard deviation

The variance σ2 is the average squared distance from the mean; the standard deviation σ is its square root, the typical distance (II.11 The shape of a residual).

The other predictors of II.10 Predict, then send the surprise fit the same scale. The 28-day average explains nothing: R2=0, since its residual is just the values moved down by a constant. No prediction at all also has R2=0 by this measure, and costs the same bits.

Takeaway
  • R2 is the share of the variance a predictor explains; the residual keeps (1−R2)σ2.
  • The previous-value predictor on the temperatures: R2≈98.4%. The average: R2=0.

From R² to bits

Combine this with the rule of II.11 The shape of a residual: bits per value ≈ log2​σ + a constant.

  1. Before prediction, the values cost log2​σ+c bits.
  2. After, the residual’s standard deviation is σ1−R2​, so it costs log2​σ+log2​1−R2​+c bits.
  3. The constant and log2​σ cancel. What is left is the saving.

☞ Things to try
  1. Drag from 0 to 50 %: half the variance gone, and only half a bit saved.
  2. Drag to 75 %: the green bell is exactly half as wide as the blue one. One bit.
  3. Watch the “next bit needs” tile as you go: 75 % → 93.8 % → 98.4 % → 99.6 %.

Check it against the measurement of II.10 Predict, then send the surprise. The formula says the previous-value predictor, at 98.4 %, saves 2.99 bits per reading; the histograms measured 6.53 − 3.63 = 2.91. Close, and the small gap has known causes (II.11 › Halve σ, save a bit): the raw temperatures are not quite bell-shaped, so their own cost was already a little below the formula’s, and counting 1,344 readings estimates it slightly low.

ConnectionThe same number as mutual information

For bell-shaped values and a straight-line predictor, 21​log2​1−R21​ is exactly the mutual information between a value and its prediction. The exchange rate is not a new idea; it is II.6 Mutual information written in the language of variances.

Takeaway
  • Bits saved per value =21​log2​1−R21​, for bell-shaped residuals.
  • 50 % → 0.5 bit, 75 % → 1 bit, 94 % → 2 bits, 99 % → 3.3 bits.
  • On the temperatures: 98.4 % explained, 2.99 bits predicted, 2.91 measured.

Why the rate is harsh

Each extra bit halves the residual’s σ, which means cutting the unexplained variance by four. The first bit needs 75 % explained. The second needs 93.75 %, the third 98.4 %, the fourth 99.6 %. Every bit asks you to remove three quarters of whatever is still unexplained.

The flip side is just as important. A predictor that gets the gist right — the season, the general trend, “it’s roughly like yesterday” — often explains a modest share of the variance, and a modest share is worth little: 30 % explained saves a quarter of a bit. Big savings come only from predictors that capture almost everything.

Running themeLossless pays for all the noise

The last few percent are usually not available at any price. At Gull Rock the sensor’s noise — about one tenth of a degree, assumed in this toy — makes up roughly 0.2 % of the readings’ variance, and no predictor can explain it. So R2 cannot pass about 99.8 %, and no predictor can save much more than 4.5 bits on these readings. Lossless coding must pay for the noise in full.

Also on II.11 · II.19 · IV.14 · V.5 · V.7 · VI.3 · VII.6 · VII.15

When the language model arrives, the same question will come back in a far more expensive setting: how much of the variance of its stored numbers could a dictionary explain? IV.14 The trap: the exchange rate is harsh and VI.3 The exchange rate, applied put numbers on it. Keep this curve in mind; it is the reason those numbers turn out modest.

CarefulWhat the formula assumes

It assumes the residual keeps the same shape as it narrows (bell-shaped before and after) and stays several integer steps wide. For fat-tailed residuals, or residuals squeezed below one step, it is only a guide — measure instead, as II.11 The shape of a residual does with Monte Carlo.

Takeaway
  • Every extra bit requires cutting the unexplained variance by a factor of four.
  • Getting the gist right explains little variance, and little variance buys little: 30 % → a quarter of a bit.
  • Unpredictable noise caps R2, and with it the bits any predictor can save.
