II.22

Reuse distance

Write one number under every request — how many other logbooks were used since this one was last needed — and you can read off how many misses an LRU desk of every size would make, without simulating any of them. A clairvoyant forecaster sets the bound no policy can beat.

The simulator of the last page answers one question at a time: this desk size, this policy, this list of requests. Move the slider and it runs the whole morning again. Is there a way to know, in one pass, how a desk of every size would do?

There is, for LRU. It rests on a single number written under every request.

One number per request

Take the storm morning and, for each request, look back to the last time the same logbook was requested. Count how many different other logbooks were used in between.

The third request, G, has distance 1: only S was used since the first G. The thirteenth, H, has distance 4: G, S, L and F came in between.

reuse distancememory, caches & hardware
  • What it is: for a request, the number of distinct other items requested since this item’s previous request. A first request has distance ∞. (In the caching literature: stack distance.)
  • The rule: under LRU with B slots, a request misses exactly when its reuse distance is at least B.
  • Easy to confuse with: the number of requests in between. G S S S G gives the second G a distance of 1, not 3.

Why the rule holds: keep the logbooks in a pile ordered by last use, most recent on top. A logbook with reuse distance u has exactly u others above it, so it sits at position u+1. An LRU desk with B slots holds exactly the top B of the pile. So the logbook is on the desk if u+1≤B, that is u<B; it misses if u≥B.

We write B for the number of slots. In Part III the same letter counts the slots of the GPU’s cache.

CarefulAn off-by-one in the first draft

The first draft said a request misses when its reuse distance exceeds B. With distance counted as above (distinct others), the correct statement is at least B: a logbook with distance 4 misses on a 4-slot desk. Off-by-ones like this are harmless at the scale of thousands of slots, but they are worth getting right once.

Takeaway
  • Reuse distance = distinct other items since the previous request for the same item (∞ for a first request).
  • LRU with B slots misses exactly the requests with reuse distance ≥B.

The miss-rate curve

The rule turns a list of distances into the miss rate of every desk size at once. Count how many requests have each distance (a histogram), then add up the tail.

m(B)=number of requestsnumber of requests with reuse distance≥B​(II.22·1)
SymbolsWhat’s in this equation (2)
m
miss rate — Fraction of requests (selections) not found in the cache; m(B) for a cache of size B. II.22
B
cache slots [items] — How many items a cache can hold. For HiSparse’s hot cache: entry slots per request per layer (2048, 4096 or 8192 in the examples). II.21
miss-rate curvememory, caches & hardware
  • What it is: the miss rate m of an LRU cache as a function of its size B.
  • How it is made: one pass over the requests to compute reuse distances; then m(B) is the fraction with distance ≥B, for every B at once.
  • Why it is one curve: a bigger LRU desk always holds everything a smaller one holds, so every request that hits on a small desk also hits on a bigger one. (This “stack” property is why the trick works for LRU and not for FIFO.)

For the storm morning the curve reads: m(1)=100%, m(2)=88%, then a cliff to m(3)=46%, m(4)=42%, m(5)=38%, and a floor of 33 % from 6 slots on. The cliff sits where the histogram has its big bar: ten requests have distance 2 (Gull Rock and Seal Point alternating around a third logbook). A desk of 3 catches all of them. The floor is the 8 first requests, which no desk can catch.

IntuitionReading a cache from its histogram

Where the reuse distances pile up, the curve drops. A cache sized just past a big bar of the histogram is a bargain; a cache sized in a gap between bars buys nothing. Part III reads a language model’s cache numbers off exactly this kind of curve (III.9 One curve behind every cache number).

Takeaway
  • m(B) = share of requests with reuse distance ≥B: the LRU miss rate of every size, from one histogram.
  • The curve drops where distances pile up and flattens at the share of first requests.

The clairvoyant forecaster

LRU guesses the future from the past. Imagine a forecaster who knows the rest of the morning’s list.

Béládymemory, caches & hardware
  • What it is: Bélády’s optimal policy (1966): when something must go, evict the item whose next request lies furthest in the future (or never comes).
  • Why it matters: no policy can make fewer misses. It is impossible to run live, because it needs the future, but on a recorded list of requests it gives a hard bound.
  • Easy to confuse with: LRU run backwards. LRU looks at the last use, Bélády at the next.

On the storm morning Bélády is only a little better than LRU: 9 misses against 10 with 4 slots, and both reach the floor of 8 from 6 slots. LRU is already nearly as good as anything could be. To miss less, the forecaster needs a bigger desk, not a cleverer rule.

Now choose the round-robin sweep in the figure: G S H L N, over and over. Every reuse distance is 4. With 4 slots, LRU evicts, every single time, exactly the logbook that will be needed next: 24 misses out of 24. Bélády, knowing the cycle, misses only 9. Add a fifth slot and both drop to the 5 first requests.

Key ideaWhat the gap tells you

The distance between LRU and Bélády on recorded requests is the most any smarter eviction policy could win. A small gap says: stop tuning the policy, the only lever left is size. Part III measures this gap on a real language model’s cache and draws exactly that kind of conclusion (III.8 The Lucky Louis property).

Takeaway
  • Bélády evicts the item needed furthest in the future: the best any policy can do.
  • On bursty requests LRU comes close (10 vs 9 misses); on a cycle just larger than the cache it fails completely (24 vs 9).
  • The LRU–Bélády gap measures what policy tuning could still gain.
