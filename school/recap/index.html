II.25

School’s out — a cheat sheet

Every idea of this Part on one page, each in one line, with the places later on the site where it does real work. Then the whole course as one map, and an invitation to practise.

That was the whole course: twenty-three small pages, from a single yes/no answer to the wire between a GPU and its host. Nothing on the rest of the site uses an idea that is not on this page or defined where it is first used.

The cheat sheet

One line per page, and where each idea does its real work later. Hover a link for the full page name.

II.2 Bits: twenty questions A bit is one yes/no answer; n bits tell 2n things apart. everywhere; VI.1 the bit budget
II.3 Surprise Rare outcomes carry more bits: surprisal =−log2​p. VI.1
II.4 Entropy: the average surprise Entropy is the average surprise — the floor for any lossless code. VI.1, VI.2 solo coding
II.5 Side information What the receiver already knows makes messages cheaper. IV.1, VII.9
II.6 Mutual information The bits saved by knowing something: H(X)−H(X∣Y). IV.12, VII.3
II.7 Codes and Huffman Prefix codes; Huffman gives each symbol about its surprisal in whole bits. V.12 KVComp
II.8 Arithmetic coding and rANS Arithmetic coding and rANS reach fractions of a bit; a wrong model costs cross-entropy. VI.10 two rates
II.9 Fixed-length codes and escapes Fixed-length codes with escapes: fast, parallel, random access, a few bits dearer. V.4 SplitZip, VI.10
II.10 Predict, then send the surprise Predict, then send only the exact residual. VI.4
II.11 The shape of a residual A bell-shaped residual costs about log2​σ + constant bits: halve σ, save a bit. VI.10
II.12 The exchange rate: variance explained → bits Explaining a share R2 of the variance saves 21​log2​1−R21​ bits: 75 % → 1 bit, 94 % → 2. VI.3, IV.14 the trap
II.13 Vectors and channels A vector is a row of d numbers; a channel is one position across many vectors — a column. III.3, VI.5 loc
II.14 Channels that move together Covariance: the average signed rectangle. The covariance matrix holds every pair. VI.6
II.15 Turning the axes (KLT) The KLT turns the axes to the data’s own directions; coordinates become uncorrelated. VI.6, V.9 KVTC
II.16 What the rotation buys A rotation saves 21​log2​(AM/GM) per element; only sharp low rank pays; lifting makes it lossless. VI.6, VII.10
II.17 Clusters and codebooks (VQ) VQ: name the nearest centroid, send the residual; the index costs log2​C bits. VI.7
II.18 Codebooks versus rotations On bell-shaped data a codebook cannot beat a rotation; only clusters let it win. VII.5, VII.13
II.19 Integers and floats INT8 is an even grid; floats are logarithmic; BF16’s low mantissa bits are noise. V.5, VII.7
II.20 Eight-bit floats and index space FP8 E4M3: 253 values up to 448. In index space, 0.1 and −0.1 are 58 steps apart. IX.5, VI.11
II.21 The forecaster's desk A cache keeps a few items close; LRU evicts the least recently used. III.6, III.7
II.22 Reuse distance Reuse distance ≥B means an LRU miss; one histogram gives m(B) for every size; Bélády bounds all policies. III.9, VII.12
II.23 Wires and memories Bandwidth vs latency; HBM is about 100× faster to reach than host DRAM over PCIe. III.5, III.10
II.24 Pay once, use many times A fixed cost per item is fixed ÷ items; a dictionary costs 8w/b bits per number. VI.14, VI.13
Running themeSide information at both ends

Look down the middle column and the same move keeps coming back: something both ends agree on in advance — the order of a vector’s elements, a table of channel means, a rotation, a codebook, a grid — makes every later message shorter. That is side information, and the rest of the site is about which side information is worth keeping where.

Takeaway
  • Information: bits, surprise, entropy, side information, mutual information.
  • Coding: codes, entropy coders, fixed-length codes, prediction and residuals, the exchange rate.
  • Vectors: channels, correlation, rotations (KLT) and codebooks (VQ), and when each one wins.
  • Numbers and machines: even and logarithmic grids, index space, caches, reuse distance, wires, amortization.

Three connections are worth saying out loud, because each was a small surprise on its page.

  • Rotation and prediction are the same redundancy. For the two thermometers, turning the axes and predicting one thermometer from the other both save 2.95 bits per reading (II.15).
  • A codebook is a taxed rotation on bell-shaped data. It can find the same structure, but it pays log2​C bits per vector for it (II.18).
  • The grid decides whether prediction pays. The same error in value is 1 step or 58 steps of FP8, depending on where it falls (II.20).
