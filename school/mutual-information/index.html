II.6

Mutual information

The bits that side information saves have a name, mutual information. Redundancy, similarity and predictability turn out to be three more names for the same number of bits.

The season almanac saved 0.16 bits per sky report. Now ask the question backwards. Suppose the harbour office lost its calendar and could only read the sky reports. How much would one report tell it about the season?

Exactly 0.16 bits.

That is no coincidence. It is a property of the number itself, and it is the first clue that this number is more fundamental than “what the almanac saves”.

Bits saved by knowing

mutual informationinformation theory

  • What it is: the number of bits that knowing Y saves when describing X:

    I(X;Y)=bits without YH(X)​​−bits with YH(X∣Y)​​(II.6·1)
    SymbolsWhat’s in this equation (4)
    I
    mutual information [bits] — Bits saved by knowing one thing when describing another: I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X∣Y). II.6
    X
    a message / random variable — The thing being described or sent (tomorrow’s weather, one value, one entry). II.4
    Y
    side information — Something the receiver already knows (the season, a dictionary). II.5
    H
    entropy [bits] — Average surprisal: the fewest bits per symbol any lossless code can average. II.4

  • Example: for the sky and the season, 1.75−1.59=0.16 bits per report.

  • Easy to confuse with: correlation. Mutual information is measured in bits, works for symbols as well as numbers, and catches any kind of dependence.

ReminderConditional entropy

H(X∣Y) is the average number of bits still needed for X when Y is known; it is never more than H(X) on average (II.5 Side information).

Now run it the other way. The season, one of four equally likely, has H(Y)=2 bits. After seeing one sky report, the season is still uncertain: on average H(Y∣X)=1.84 bits remain (a fog report hints at winter, a sunny one at summer, but only hints). The difference is 2−1.84=0.16 bits — the same number. Mutual information is symmetric: what the season tells about the sky equals what the sky tells about the season.

The figure draws this as two overlapping bars, and lets you swap the season for other kinds of side information.

☞ Things to try
  1. Nothing: the bottom bar vanishes and all 1.75 bits must be sent.
  2. The report itself: the bars coincide. Knowing X saves all of H(X).
  3. The previous report, stickiness 0: no overlap. Stickiness 0.8: about 1 bit saved. 0.95: nearly 1.5.
Takeaway
  • Mutual information I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X∣Y) is the number of bits that knowing Y saves.
  • It is symmetric: H(Y)−H(Y∣X) gives the same number.
  • It is 0 exactly when X and Y are independent, and at most the entropy of either.

Three names, one number

People describe the same saving in at least three everyday ways. On this site all three mean mutual information, in bits.

redundancyinformation theory
  • What it is: the bits a message spends on things the receiver could have predicted.
  • Example: a sky report coded with the year-round code spends 0.16 bits per report on what the season already said, for a receiver who knows the season.
  • Easy to confuse with: repetition. Redundancy need not look like repeated bytes; any predictability counts.
everyday phrase what it means here in bits
“the reports are redundant given the season” the reports spend bits on what the season predicts I(X;Y)
“today’s sky is similar to the last report” knowing one makes the other cheaper to send I(X;Y)
“the sky is predictable from the season” the season narrows down the sky I(X;Y)
ReminderOrder-0 entropy

The entropy computed from symbol frequencies alone, ignoring which symbols came before. For the sky it is 1.75 bits.

The figure’s second example makes the point sharper. With an assumed stickiness of 0.8, the previous report saves 1.01 bits per sky report — six times what the season saves. Half an hour ago is a much closer neighbour than “it is summer”. The order-0 entropy of II.4 Entropy: the average surprise ignores order, so it leaves this whole bit on the table.

Watch the direction of the arrows, because it is easy to get backwards:

When the side information becomes more useful…
…these go up
  • mutual information I(X;Y)
  • redundancy, similarity, predictability (the same bits by other names)
…these go down
  • conditional entropy H(X∣Y)
  • the bits per report on the radio link

The first draft of this project mixed up exactly these two columns (the story is told in VII.3 Predictability is mutual information, not its reverse).

Takeaway
  • Redundancy, similarity and predictability are mutual information under other names, and are measured in bits.
  • More of them means less conditional entropy, and fewer bits on the wire.
  • The closest neighbour (the previous report) knows far more than a broad context (the season).

What “never hurts” does not promise

II.5 Side information showed that conditioning never increases entropy on average. That is a theorem. It is also easy to over-read. Four pieces of fine print:

  1. On average, not every time. In winter the almanac makes the sky harder to guess (1.98 bits against 1.75). Only the average over seasons is guaranteed to fall.
  2. Only if the receiver really has Y. A summer-coded report is gibberish to an office without the almanac.
  3. Only for the true probabilities. A coder working with wrong numbers can lose bits; II.8 Arithmetic coding and rANS prices that mistake.
  4. Delivering Y is not free. The almanac had to be printed, shipped and kept.
Running themeEvery helper charges a tax

Point 4 is this theme in miniature. The almanac saves 0.16 bits per report, but it costs something to get it to both ends. Here it is printed once and used for years, so its cost per report is tiny. That will not always be the case — Part I’s trained dictionary had to be shipped too, and the question “is the saving bigger than the tax?” will follow every kind of side information on this site.

Takeaway
  • Conditioning lowers entropy on average, not in every case.
  • The guarantee needs the side information at the receiver and correct probabilities.
  • Getting side information to the receiver has its own cost, which the saving must beat.
