The profile of a dictionary problem

Two conditions turn a pile of data into a dictionary problem. This page states them, maps the Gull Rock story onto the twelve Parts of the site, and puts the old words next to ours.

Gull Rock taught us a recipe. Before following it into an LLM, write down exactly when it applies. Otherwise “use a dictionary” becomes a hammer, and everything starts looking like a nail.

Two conditions

The profile of a dictionary problem
something to learnalike items​​+no context in any onetiny fetches​​⇓a dictionary is the tool​

Both conditions must hold. Drop either one and something simpler wins.

  1. An ensemble of alike items. The items share structure that a compressor could learn: the same skeleton, the same typical values, the same habits. Measured properly, “alike” means the items together carry far fewer bits than each one separately (II.6 Mutual information makes this precise).
  2. Tiny, independent fetches. Each item is written, sent or read on its own, so no single fetch carries enough context for ordinary compression (I.4 › Lesson 1: the redundancy was always there).

The four combinations, with examples:

read as one big stream fetched as tiny, independent pieces
items alike an ordinary compressor on the stream does it — Gull Rock’s daily log: 13 bytes per message a dictionary problem — Gull Rock’s radio: 125 bytes per message alone, 22 with a dictionary
items unrelated little helps — already-compressed photos, encrypted files nothing helps — no dictionary can describe what the items do not share

The rest of this site asks whether the memory of a sparse-attention LLM (Part III calls it the orchard) sits in the bold cell. Condition 2 is a plain fact about how such an LLM reads its memory: small pieces, scattered, a few at every step (III.5 Sparse attention and the indexer). Condition 1 was the open question when this project began: nobody had measured how alike those pieces are in bits. Part VIII reports what two measurement campaigns found (VIII.1 A dispatch from the harness).

Running themeThe access pattern decides the form

Here the access pattern is one of the two conditions. The same alike items call for an ordinary compressor when read as a stream, and for a dictionary when fetched one by one.

Also on I.4 · II.9 · II.23 · III.5 · IV.2 · IV.9 · V.11 · VI.5 · VI.9 · VIII.3 · VIII.4 · VIII.9 · VIII.13 · IX.1 · IX.3 · IX.9 · X.7
Takeaway
  • A dictionary is the tool when items are alike and fetched as tiny, independent pieces.
  • Alike but streamed: use an ordinary compressor. Not alike: nothing helps.
  • For an LLM’s memory, condition 2 is known; condition 1 had to be measured, and Part VIII reports the result.

The first draft of this site used words loosely, each meaning whatever its source paper meant by it, and several of them meant two things at once. Almost all of these pages lie ahead; this table is a promise that every word will be explained before it is leaned on, and a place to come back to when a hover reminder is not enough. Two words on this page already have their meaning: dictionary is anything both ends hold in advance (a zstd dictionary, a few typical values, later a codebook or a set of reference cherries are all kinds of it), and delta is what one item travels as, given the dictionary. Two more things the first draft got wrong were ideas, not words. “Similarity”, “redundancy” and “mutual information” are one quantity, in bits, and more of it means more predictable; the draft had the direction backwards (II.6 Mutual information, VII.1 Bits are the unit). And the length of a stretch is a knob we choose, not a property of the data (IV.3 The stretch: a knob, not a fact).

CarefulWhy fuss over words?

Because two of the rows above hide conclusions, not just style. One old word, “entry”, stood for what the indexer selects and for what the compressor shrinks; those are different objects, with different sizes, and a bit count for one is not a bit count for the other. Another, “block”, was a range of tokens in one sentence and a box of data in the next, which hid the fact that the range is ours to choose. Part IV gives each object its own word and its own line of Python.

Takeaway
  • Every old word gets one precise replacement here, with a page that defines it.
  • Dictionary is the general idea; a codebook, a set of typical values and a zstd dictionary are kinds of it.
  • Similarity, redundancy and mutual information are one quantity, measured in bits.
