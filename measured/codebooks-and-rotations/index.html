VIII.7

Codebooks and rotations

The Hive and the Compass of Part V went on trial as real compressors. Every stored codebook lost to pointing at an earlier cherry, every fixed rotation lost to predicting without rotating, and each loss has a reason that fits in one sentence.

On 30 October an encoder was handed a gift. Alongside its reference tree and its linear prediction table it was offered a codebook of 4, 16 or 64 typical cherries, free to use or to ignore, and told to pick whatever cost the fewest bits. On 72 punnets out of 72 it picked nothing.

This page is the story of why, and of a cousin result: three rotations that lost by a quarter of a bit each. The two stage families of Part V that store a fitted object, the Hive’s centroids (V.5 VQ: the Hive, at work) and the Compass’s basis (V.4 KLT: the Compass, at work), both lost to things the punnet already had. The human had asked for exactly this trial, suspicions set aside.

Codebooks on trial

ReminderThe Hive, and the toolbox’s bound (V.5)

VQ names, for each cherry, the nearest of C centroids stored in a codebook, then codes the exact residual. The index costs log2​C bits per cherry (the index tax); the codebook costs 8C/b bits per element. On bell-shaped data the toolbox says VQ can save at most what a free rotation saves plus its own index tax, and ends with “whether cherries cluster is an open measurement”.

The measurement came in. Campaign C2 first computed bounds for this branch. Then the human ruled that here a bound is only a lead and a real compressor answers, so the whole family was built for real: VQ, PQ, RQ (a codebook, then a second one for what the first leaves), OPQ (PQ after a learned rotation), binary codes and LSH (a few bits per cherry, taken from signs).

Each quantiser was stacked on linear prediction with no tree, on the layer screen (V · all · cherry v[t, l, :, :] · stretch 4096 · leash 293). RQ saved 0.0029 of MDL ratio on all 14 parts of the screen and passed the separate decoder; PQ saved 0.0017 (quick). Both stayed about 0.05 behind a tree compressor on the same screen. Stacked on tree and prediction, the encoder chose no codebook on 72 of 72 punnets (quick). Why? Put the two things a cherry can be predicted from side by side.

A codeword versus a reference cherry
A codeword
  • Fitted to the punnet and stored only to help others: its e bytes are pure tax, 8C/b bits per element.
  • Each cherry that uses it pays log2​C bits to name it.
  • The average of a cluster: close to many cherries, identical to none.
A reference cherry
  • A real earlier cherry of the punnet, the reference cherry. It must be stored anyway, so using it costs only a pointer, about 12 bits uncoded.
  • When the text repeats, a near copy, where a centroid is only an average.
  • The more references, the better: on one punnet per KV layer (compressor 102’s probability table, real bytes, encoder bytes) the ratio fell at every step from 64 references to the whole tree.
references per punnet KV layer 0 (L3) KV layer 1 (L7) KV layer 4 (L19)
64 0.6858 0.6838 0.7015
256 0.6518 0.6703 0.6841
the whole tree 0.5737 0.6275 0.6403
Running themePay once, use many times

The Hive’s codebook is this theme’s classic fixed cost, paid by the b cherries of its punnet. A reference tree’s “codebook” is the punnet’s own cherries, already paid for in full; what is left to amortize is one pointer per cherry.

Also on I.4 · II.24 · IV.6 · IV.10 · V.4 · VI.2 · VI.4 · VI.8 · VIII.9 · XI.9

Do values cluster at all? In a minority of cherries. On one KV layer 0 punnet, RQ cut the mean delta from 360 to 348 bytes, and 42 % of that saving came from the top tenth of cherries: a codebook shrinks a few cherries a lot and leaves the rest alone. PCA, OPQ, binary codes and LSH left the distribution where it was: they act on no cluster.

Takeaway
  • Built as real compressors, the codebooks gained at most 0.0029 (RQ) on prediction alone; stacked on a tree, none was ever chosen (72 of 72 punnets).
  • A codeword is stored only to help others; a reference cherry is stored anyway and costs a pointer. The toolbox’s bound held, and the reason now has a name.
  • Clusters exist, in a minority of cherries.

Rotations on trial

ReminderThe Compass (V.4, V.9)

The KLT rotates the axes to the data’s own directions and codes the coordinates one by one; its gain is large only for a sharply spiky spectrum. To be lossless it needs lifting: integer shears that can be undone exactly (V.9 Lifting: how a rotation becomes lossless).

The campaign tried the fixed rotations of transform coding (the DCT of JPEG, the Hadamard transform, a random rotation) as code-length estimates on two heads, KV layers 0 and 2 (the LLM’s 3 and 11). All three behaved as one transform, agreeing within 0.002, and all three lost at every quantisation step, by +0.28 to +0.32 bit per element at best. Two reasons, each enough on its own.

  1. Rotating then rounding has a price. Rotate a vector of integers and round, and the data sits on a new square grid in many dimensions. That rounding costs 0.2546 bit per coordinate, the lattice constant; the campaign measured 0.29–0.32. Linear prediction subtracts a guess from each element and never rounds the vector, so it never pays.
  2. Element numbers carry no neighbourhood. A DCT assumes that position 17 of a vector is related to position 18. In a value cherry the mean correlation between elements at the same index equals the mean over any pair (0.0734 at KV layer 2). Sorting the elements does not create what is not there.

V.4 KLT: the Compass, at work leaves the lifted rotation as a question for the harness. Lifting makes a rotation reversible; it does not make the rounding free, and that tax, a quarter of a bit per element, is about the whole gain the Compass was expected to make on “low-rank-ish” data (0.34 bit).

Takeaway
  • DCT, Hadamard and random rotations lose +0.28–0.32 bit per element (code-length estimate, two heads).
  • Rounding after a rotation costs about 0.25 bit per element; prediction never rounds the vector, so it never pays.
  • Element numbers carry no neighbourhood, so a DCT has nothing to work with.

The light compressor

What does this branch look like done well, with no tree at all? Compressor 129: per furrow a mean and a scale, a sparse linear-prediction table over the whole cherry, one coded stream per cherry, and a dictionary share xD​ of only 0.01–0.04. Real and verified.

cherry shape compressor 129 dictionary share
one head, v[t, l, h, :] 0.7348 0.010
one KV layer, v[t, l, :, :] 0.6863 0.021
two KV layers 0.6696
three KV layers by kind (0+4+5, 1+2+3) 0.6667
three adjacent KV layers 0.6652
one token, v[t, :, :, :] 0.6622 0.042

(Different screens, so read the shape; the campaign itself calls the comparison good to ±0.005.)

Against 102’s 0.6449 on the same layer screen, 129 is 0.041 worse on all 14 parts of the screen. That gap is what the text’s repeats are worth once prediction has taken the geometry. Two trends sit behind the table. The tree adds less as cherries widen, because other parts of a wide cherry do the job a reference would. Stretch length runs the other way: from 4096 to 16 384 tokens the light compressor stops improving while the tree keeps going.

For kvblockdiff's miss path, 129 is the compressor that matches the application’s own picture (VI.2 How big is each piece?): a tiny dictionary, and every miss a delta of about 0.66–0.69 of a raw cherry. The tree compressors look nothing like it; VIII.9 Reading one cherry shows what they look like instead.

CarefulThe branch is not closed

Of the close-enough family’s 62 techniques, 21 now have a real compressor, 18 only a bound, 16 are untried and 7 do not apply. Most quantiser rows are quick (only RQ without a tree passed the separate decoder), none exists at a stretch of 16 384 or on v[t, :, :, :] cherries, where a codebook is cheaper per cherry, and all were stacked on one prediction design.

Takeaway
  • Compressor 129, with no tree, runs from 0.7348 on v[t, l, h, :] to 0.6622 on v[t, :, :, :] with a 1–4 % dictionary; it sits 0.041 behind the tree compressor on v[t, l, :, :] cherries.
  • The tree matters less as cherries widen and more as stretches grow.
  • 21 of 62 techniques have a real compressor; the rest are bounds or untried.
