VIII.12

The scorecard

Parts V to VII wrote their guesses down so that the data could contradict them. Here is the ledger, line by line: what the site said, what the campaigns measured, the verdict. Two of the hypotheses come out changed, one new one is the strongest structure found, and the stage families finish in an order nobody predicted.

A forecaster who writes the forecast down before looking out of the window has to show it afterwards. Part V’s toolbox wrote its expectations for each stage family, and Part VII stated each hypothesis in bits. This page holds both against what came back, one line per prediction.

Predictions, scored

Every measured number below is pinned on an earlier page of this Part; the verdicts are the Part’s, and a reader who disagrees with one can follow the link and argue.

Count the verdicts. Six confirmed, one of them with its reason supplied by the data and two of them the new hypotheses of Part VII. Two wrong, one half wrong, one small. Three turned out easier or larger than the site feared. Two remain open because the campaign measured something adjacent to what the application asks for: it compressed with entropy coders when the store wants fixed-length deltas, and it never ran an indexer.

Takeaway
  • Solo, the grid, the VQ bound, stereo, echo and the leash came out as predicted; the value prior, the rotation plan and the fallen-apple curve did not.
  • The gains are real, in values the napkin wrote off, from mechanisms (repeats, weight geometry) the first draft did not name.
  • Two of the application’s engineering numbers, the fixed-length rate and the miss path, were never measured.

The hypotheses and the cast

Part VII’s hypotheses each get their verdict, then the stage families get their curtain call.

The Lucky Louis property: untested here. The campaigns measured bytes. They never ran an indexer, never recorded a pick, never asked which positions a decode step wants. Whether picks repeat, and whether misses pile into a few heavy stretches, stands exactly where HiSparse left it (III.9 The Lucky Louis property, VII.8 Lucky Louis, stretch by stretch).

Fallen-apple similarity: half wrong. The hypothesis drew a curve over token distance Δ, cheapest next door and fading with distance. In the data, the adjacent token is nearly useless at KV layers 0, 4 and 5 (0.058 bit of mutual information per element at KV layer 0, against 0.31 at KV layers 1 and 2), and the reference cherries that pay are the same token again, often far away in the punnet. Distance does matter in the middle KV layers, where 37–47 % of references sit within 8 tokens against 7–12 % elsewhere, and where values are smooth along the text (VIII.8 Sound, time and bits).

Echo similarity: the axis that mattered. The term the first draft called “a floor that never fades” turned out to be the main one. Hubs of far-apart repeats at the first and last KV layers, chains in the middle; the reference tree lives on echoes.

Stereo similarity: the strongest structure measured. Joining the two heads of a KV layer saves 0.0322 of ratio on 20 of 20 sequences, 0.85 bit per element shared raw, at a cosine near zero (VIII.6 Cutting the orchard: cherry shapes compared).

Upstairs similarity: small, and by kind. Joining all six KV layers into one cherry saves 0.0043 beyond joining the two heads. Given everything else, adjacent layers share 0.03–0.08 bit per element. And the layers that help each other are of the same kind, KV layers 0, 4 and 5 together (the LLM’s 3, 19, 23), 1, 2 and 3 together (7, 11, 15), a grouping that beat nearest-neighbour grouping by 0.0015 on 7 of 7 sequences. The staircase has landings, and they are grouped by what the layer does rather than by which floor it is on.

Then the cast, one line each.

  • The Twin (sibling) won. A reference tree is a sibling dictionary with the pointer made cheap and the siblings chosen well; the twin ladder of VIII.4 Night one: the twin ladder runs from 0.80 to 0.67 on one head.
  • The Hive (VQ) lost. A codeword is stored only to help others, while a reference cherry must be stored anyway, so a codebook can only add a tax; stacked on a tree the encoder chose no codebook on 72 of 72 punnets (VIII.7 Codebooks and rotations).
  • The Compass (KLT) lost. Rotating leaves the grid, and rounding back costs about 0.25 bit per element; linear prediction gets the same geometry without rounding anything.
  • The Anchor (furrow statistics) is the floor. Per-furrow statistics are the solo baseline every gain is counted from, and nothing more.
What we guessed, what we measured
What we guessed

Light dictionaries first; most of the gain in a few key furrows; a curve that fades with distance; VQ to be measured before it is trusted; ρ around 0.75–0.85, with values adding little.

What we measured

The heaviest “dictionary” won, half the punnet, made of the cherries themselves; values alone compress to 0.62–0.63; repeats beat distance; codebooks lose to real cherries; the light compressor gives ρ ≈ 0.66–0.69 and the tree compressor goes far lower.

Running themeWhich picks, versus what is inside them

The campaigns answered “what is inside the cherries” and left “which picks are wanted” untouched. The distinction that opened this site is exactly the line along which the campaign’s scope was drawn.

Takeaway
  • Lucky Louis untested, fallen-apple half wrong (repeats, with distance only in the middle layers), echo and stereo confirmed, upstairs small and grouped by kind.
  • The Twin won, the Hive and the Compass lost for reasons the data names, the Anchor is the floor.
  • The bet on bytes paid, in a way the bet did not foresee.
