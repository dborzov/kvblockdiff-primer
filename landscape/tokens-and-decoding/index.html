III.1

How an LLM writes

An LLM writes one small piece of text at a time, and before every piece it looks back at everything written so far. This page names the pieces, the layers they pass through, the vector that carries them, and the clock that times each step.

Type “The weather on Gull Rock is” into your phone and it suggests a next word. Foggy, perhaps, or grey. Accept the suggestion and it suggests another. A large LLM does exactly this, with a far better sense of what comes next. It never writes a whole sentence at once. It writes one small piece, adds it to the text, and asks itself again what comes next.

Everything this project worries about comes from that loop. So this Part goes through it slowly, in Python, and fixes a word and a shape for each part as it goes.

Tokens and the sequence

The pieces an LLM reads and writes are tokens. A token is a word, or a piece of a word. Common words are one token each; rare words get split. Here is how a tokenizer might cut the sentence, with each token’s position:

token_pos_id 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
token The ␣weather ␣on ␣Gull ␣Rock ␣is ␣fog gy

(The split is illustrative; every tokenizer cuts differently, and the space usually travels with the word that follows it.)

Each token has a position, token_pos_id, counted from 0; in equations it is t. The list of all tokens one conversation has seen, from its first token to the one being written now, is a sequence. A serving system runs many sequences at once, and each one is its own context. (On this site “sequence” never means the order of anything else.) The number of tokens a sequence holds is its context length, L: the prompt counts, and so does everything the LLM has written since. In the setting selected now a sequence holds 100 384 tokens; a chat message is a few hundred, an hour of a coding agent’s work is tens of thousands, and the two sequences this site leans on are 100 384 tokens (GLM-5.1, HiSparse’s trace) and about 225 000 tokens (Qwen3.5-2B, the campaigns’ transcripts).

The thing doing the writing is the LLM, and on this site it is always named, because two of them appear and their shapes differ at every turn: GLM-5.1, a large production LLM, and Qwen3.5-2B, a small open one that can be measured on a desk.

Takeaway
  • A token is a word or a piece of one; token_pos_id counts tokens from 0.
  • A sequence is one conversation’s tokens so far; its context length is L, about 100K and 225K tokens in the two settings.
  • The LLM is always named: GLM-5.1 or Qwen3.5-2B.

Layers and the hidden state

A token’s list of numbers does not become a prediction in one go. It is refined in a stack of stages called layers, each numbered by layer_id. Each layer reads the vector, adds a little understanding, and passes it up. The vector that climbs is the token’s hidden state, x:

IntuitionFloors of a building

Picture a building with one floor per layer. Each token is a visitor who enters at the ground floor and climbs. On every floor someone adds a note to the visitor’s file. At the top the file is complete, and the LLM reads off its guess for the next token.

The important part for us: on every floor, a token leaves something behind. Later tokens will come back to look at it (the next page says why and how). In most LLMs every layer does this. In hybrid LLMs only some layers do, and those are the KV layers: the layers that keep a key and a value for every token. The other layers carry a fixed-size state and have nothing per token to store. The layer_id of the orchard counts KV layers only, and NL​ is their number.

the LLM layers KV layers NL​ role on this site
GLM-5.1 78 78 (every layer) the deployment the numbers are pinned to (III.11 Two reference settings: the Pin and the Bench)
Qwen3.5-2B 24 6 (its layers 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, 23 are layer_id 0 … 5) the one the campaigns measured (VIII.2 The data on the table)

Takeaway
  • The hidden state x is the vector a token carries between layers; each layer refines it.
  • A KV layer keeps something per token; layer_id counts KV layers only: 78 on the Pin, 6 of Qwen3.5-2B’s 24 on the Bench.
  • A sequence has left NL​×L cells behind: millions of them.

Prefill, then decode

A sequence runs in two phases, prefill and decode.

Two phases of one sequence
Prefill
  • Reads the whole prompt at once.
  • All prompt tokens climb the layers together, in parallel.
  • Leaves the prompt’s cells at every KV layer.
  • Happens once per sequence.
Decode
  • Writes the answer one token per step.
  • Each step: one new token climbs all the layers, and at every KV layer it looks back at the cells of all earlier tokens.
  • Adds one new cell per KV layer.
  • Repeats until the answer is done: thousands of steps.

Decode is the phase this whole site is about. It is sequential: step t+1 needs the token written at step t. And every step looks back at more material than the one before.

☞ Things to try
  1. Step to the end and compare the last two read-outs. How many more cells does the last step look at than the one before?
  2. Imagine 100 384 columns and 78 rows. Each decode step looks at every cell to its left, on every row.

How long may one step take? The time a sequence waits for each new token is the time per output token, TPOT. In the deployment the Pin is built on it is 30 ms: about 33 tokens per second, a little faster than most people read. In the setting selected now it is 30 ms (the Bench has no decode loop, so it has no step time).

The clock of decoding

Everything one step needs, every number read and every byte moved, must fit into this window, for every sequence the machine serves at once.

Takeaway
  • Prefill reads the prompt in one go; decode then writes one token per step.
  • Every decode step looks back at what all earlier tokens left at every KV layer, so the pile it consults grows with every step.
  • The step time TPOT (30 ms on the Pin) is the budget everything else must fit into.
