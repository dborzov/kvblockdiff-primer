IX.14

What would be new

Lay every paper of this Part on one grid and one square stays empty. Exact coding of 8-bit orchards that uses what cherries share, measured with controls, on the scattered-read path of sparse decoding. Three claims, each with its evidence, its caveat, and what Part VIII has already delivered on it.

Six papers on these pages, a few more on the neighbouring ones, and each answered a question near ours. Put them side by side on the questions that matter for kvblockdiff, and look for the column nobody fills. Then look at what Part VIII already wrote into it.

Read the table by columns. Two kinds of evidence exist in the literature, and they do not meet.

  • Lossy papers show where structure lives: key furrows with sizes of their own (KIVI, KVQuant), a few dominant directions (KVTC), atoms shared by all texts (Lexico), neighbours alike within a furrow (CacheGen), and what the LLM tolerates. Their yardstick is accuracy at a memory budget.
  • Lossless compressors count bits, but without context: SplitZip prices each number by its own histogram (an order-0 bound of 6.19 bits on E5M2), and the float compressors do the same for exponents.

Nobody in print converts “where structure lives” into “bits saved for an exact 8-bit copy”. The conversion needs one number per predictor, the variance it explains on the stored grid, and the exchange rate is harsh: 75 % for one bit, 94 % for two (V.12 The exchange rate, applied). The campaigns’ row is the first with every column filled but the last, and the last is filled by HiSparse’s row above it.

Running themeWhich picks, versus what is inside them

The same confusion three times over. HiSparse’s hit rate says which picks will be wanted, not what is inside them. Lossy papers say what the LLM tolerates, not what is predictable. Only a measurement of bits for an exact copy says what is inside, and Part VIII is the first.

Takeaway
  • Lossy work locates structure; lossless work counts bits without context. The two never meet in print.
  • The empty square: shared bits of 8-bit orchards, with controls, on the scattered-read path. Part VIII filled it for values of one LLM.

Three claims

1. A measurement: shared bits at 8 bits, with controls

  • Claim. How many bits per element a cherry saves given what it shares with the rest of its punnet, along each axis of the orchard: tokens (fallen-apple similarity), layers (upstairs similarity), heads (stereo similarity), repeats (echo similarity), measured on the stored 8-bit grid with a shuffle control, a synthetic control and an exact round trip.
  • Evidence it was missing. CacheGen’s 2.4–2.9× variance ratio was the closest thing in print, with an unclear baseline. KVFetcher’s ratio bundles rounding with coding. SplitZip stops at order-0.
  • What Part VIII delivered. On the Bench’s values (Qwen3.5-2B, FP8 E4M3, agent transcripts): solo 0.778; the best verified compressor 0.636 at stretch 4096, 1.1 bits per element over solo; the two heads share 0.85 bit per element at a cosine of 0.05 (VII.5 Stereo similarity (head axis)); far repeats of the same token, not neighbours, are what references point at (VII.6 Echo similarity (repeats)); adjacent layers share 0.03–0.08 bit (VII.4 Upstairs similarity (layer axis)); the shuffle control separated the text’s repeats from the LLM’s geometry (VIII.5 Two kinds of structure).
  • Honest caveat. One LLM, one kind of text, values only, mostly 2–5 sequences. Keys, prose, a second LLM and the sequence scope are open (X.1 Where we stand). A small or negative result on any of them is still a result.

2. A data path: an exact, random-access delta store for the miss path

  • Claim. Keep the orchard as punnet dictionaries plus deltas, so a miss moves a delta and the GPU rebuilds the cherry bit for bit, cold, under the leash (VI.1 From the scenario to the serving system).
  • Evidence it is missing. Every lossless orchard compressor we found is built for bulk transfers or streams: SplitZip (prefill to decode), KVFetcher and CacheGen (network fetch), KVTC (offload with DEFLATE). HiSparse moves raw picks.
  • What Part VIII delivered. The leash is cheaper than feared: a cold decode within 293 cherries’ worth cost nothing at stretch 4096 and above; at 137 it cost 0.023 of ratio; the worst cherry reads 1.1–3.7× the mean (VIII.9 Reading one cherry). A layout along reference-tree paths made a dictionary share of 0.5 cheap to read.
  • Honest caveat. The parts are known: fixed-length codes, escapes, reference cherries, punnet dictionaries, even CacheGen’s reference-per-stretch layout. Only the combination and the target are new. No fixed-length rate and no GPU decode time have been measured (X.7 How fast must the decoder be?).

3. Economics: where misses land, and what residency costs

  • Claim. Whether the indexer’s misses pile into a few long-lived heavy stretches, and what dictionaries do to sequences per GPU once their loads and their HBM are paid (VI.9 The napkin for on fire, VI.8 On fire and ice: a shelf at stretch level).
  • Evidence it is missing. HiSparse reports miss rates per pick (30 / 13.4 / 6.7 %), not where the misses fall by stretch. No paper prices dictionary residency.
  • What Part VIII delivered. Nothing: no indexer ran on the Bench (VII.8 Lucky Louis, stretch by stretch). The light no-tree compressor (0.686, dictionary share 0.021) and the tree compressors (dictionary share 0.43–0.54) give the two ends of the residency question numbers to plug in (VI.7 The two boring corners, VIII.13 What it means for kvblockdiff).
  • Honest caveat. It depends on the selector, the LLM and the trace; the plan simulates the indexer with exact top-k (X.5 Queries and picks: a stand-in indexer). If light dictionaries suffice, residency hardly matters.
Takeaway
  • Claim 1 is partly delivered, for values of one LLM; it carries the other two and is publishable either way.
  • Claim 2 is new as a combination; the leash’s cost is now known to be small.
  • Claim 3 is unmeasured and matters only if dictionaries turn out heavy.

What could make us wrong

We checked every paper of this Part through abstracts, code repositories and search summaries, and a few full texts; arxiv.org itself was mostly out of reach. So the empty square is empty as far as we could see. While checking facts, search results also showed newer titles that sit close to ours and that we have not read:

From the literatureFive unread titles
  • DeltaKV: Residual-Based KV Cache Compression via Long-Range Similarity (arXiv:2602.08005, 2026). By its title and summary, predicts tokens from similar distant tokens and stores residuals. Close to claim 1 and to echo similarity, which the campaigns found to be the axis that mattered.
  • KV Cache Compression Through the Lens of Transform Coding (arXiv:2608.14191, 2026). Close to the KVTC page and the KLT bound.
  • A JoLT for the KV cache: Near-Lossless KV Cache Compression via Joint Rank-bit Allocation (arXiv:2607.12550, 2026). “Near-lossless” is the phrase to check.
  • PackKV (arXiv:2512.24449, 2025) and Codec-Gauge (arXiv:2607.20538, 2026). Lossy, by their titles.

Any of them could narrow claim 1 or 2. Reading them is the first item of homework before any write-up.

Running themeTrust, but shuffle

A claim of “nobody has measured this” is only as good as the measurement that follows it. The shuffle control is what turned CacheGen-style observations into a number on the Bench: the reference-tree gain fell from 0.69 to 0.75 of ratio when tokens were shuffled across texts, and the prediction gain did not move.
