XII.1

The one-page summary

The whole site on one page in its own words. The problem in five sentences, the application, every hypothesis with its verdict and its number, the economics without flinching, and the plan. Every line links to the page that works it out.

Parts I to X build one idea from a story about tiny files; Part VIII reports what two research campaigns measured when the idea met real data. This page is the short version, in the site’s vocabulary. Every claim has a number, and every number has a page.

The problem, in five sentences

A sender holds the orchard of one sequence, v.shape == (225_000, 6, 2, 256) on the Bench, and a receiver asks for one cherry at a time, at random positions, in an order nobody can predict (IV.2 One cherry at a time). Before any ask, the receiver may hold one dictionary per punnet, the set of all cherries of one stretch under one slice and one cherry shape (IV.6 The punnet: what one dictionary serves). Each ask is answered with a delta, and the cherry must come back bit for bit, decoded cold: decompress(deltas[i], dictionary) == punnet[i] (IV.7 The contract). The score is the MDL ratio, dictionary bytes plus delta bytes over the punnet’s raw bytes, so a dictionary is never free (IV.8 The score: the MDL ratio). The leash caps the dictionary bytes one cold decode may touch, 137 cherries’ worth in the baseline scenario, so that stream compression of the whole punnet cannot win by hiding in the dictionary (IV.9 The leash).

A scenario is one full specification, Scenario(slice, cherry_shape, stretch_len, leash, fmt, llm, corpus), and a compressor is one attempt at it. The harness is the referee that charges every byte, checks every cherry in a fresh process and pairs scores on the same punnets (IV.13 The harness: the referee).

Takeaway
  • One cherry at a time, at random, decoded cold, with a dictionary held in advance; the score charges the dictionary; the leash keeps stream compression out.
  • Scenario = the problem; compressor = one attempt; harness = the referee.

The application

HiSparse keeps each sequence’s orchard in host memory and a hot cache of raw picks per layer on the GPU; every miss moves one raw pick over the the link, 640 B on the Pin (III.7 HiSparse: a library with a reading desk). kvblockdiff maps the scenario onto that system: a pick is one or more cherries, a stretch’s dictionary lives on the dictionary shelf, and a miss whose stretch is on fire is a δ-miss that moves ρe bytes instead of e (VI.1 From the scenario to the serving system, VI.3 The shelf, and two kinds of miss). Hits, evictions and the LLM’s outputs do not change.

Two levers decide whether it pays: cheaper misses raise what the link can feed; cheaper hot-cache slots raise what GPU memory can hold; the smaller of the two is the number of sequences per GPU (VI.6 What "better than HiSparse" means). Which scenario an application needs depends on how its indexer picks: DeepSeek-V4’s per-layer picks map to one-layer cherries, GLM-5.1’s shared selection to multi-layer cherries or a dictionary shared across layers (VI.13 Which application, which scenario).

Takeaway
  • A δ-miss moves a delta; the dictionary waits on the shelf; nothing the LLM sees changes.
  • Sequences per GPU = min(what the link can feed, what GPU memory can hold); the two levers move one each.

The hypotheses and their verdicts

Every number below is on values of Qwen3.5-2B in FP8, stretches of 4096 tokens, unless the row says otherwise (VIII.3 The rules of the game, as scenarios). The floor every dictionary must beat is solo coding, 0.778 (V.2 Solo: the Hermit).

The headline: the best stretch-scope compressor on v[t, l, :, :] cherries scores 0.636 (dictionary share 0.525, delta share 0.112, worst cold decode 226 cherries’ worth of reads), the light no-tree compressor 0.686 with a dictionary of 0.021, and bigger stretches always won: 0.689, 0.636, 0.618 at 512, 4096, 16 384 tokens, no ceiling in sight (VIII.12 The scorecard, VIII.9 Reading one cherry). The dictionary share does not measure sharing; what is shared inside a punnet is about a fifth of the bits (VIII.14 What the half says, and what it does not).

Running themeWhich picks, versus what is inside them

That the indexer keeps wanting the same picks says nothing about whether their bytes are alike. The campaigns measured the second thing and found it true for reasons the proposal had not named: repeats of the same token, and a geometry fixed by the LLM’s weights.

The economics, honestly

CarefulWhat the numbers say on the Pin

HiSparse serves about 109 sequences per GPU at B=4096 slots, bound by the link; GPU memory would hold 147 (III.12 Three numbers and a deadline).

  • The light compressor (ρ≈0.69, dictionary 2 % of the punnet) is the proposal’s own picture. Fit once per sequence, lever 1 alone buys about 1/ρ​≈1.2× at each system’s best cache size. Kept per stretch with every dictionary resident, it serves fewer sequences than HiSparse, about 0.88×, bound by memory (VIII.13 What it means for kvblockdiff, VI.10 A what-if calculator).
  • The tree compressor (ρ=0.112, dictionary 0.525) keeps half the punnet on the GPU as reference cherries; a cold decode reads about 120 cherries’ worth, so the dictionary must be resident. Stacked on the hot cache it loses: 47 sequences with four stretches on fire per layer, 96 with one. As lever 2, the dictionary standing in for its stretch’s slots, about 120, or 1.1×, with one stretch on fire, an assumed δ-miss share of 0.3 and one load per 100 steps (VI.9 The napkin for on fire).
  • In dollars: at an assumed USD 3 per GPU-hour, HiSparse costs about USD 0.23 per million output tokens; 138 sequences would cost 0.18; the ratio, 0.79, holds at any price (VI.11 From bits to dollars).

Every ratio here is entropy-coded; the fixed-length rate a GPU decoder would want was never measured, and the gap is about one bit per element (V.11 Two rates: entropy and fixed-length). The δ-miss share fD​, the load rate nload​ and how long a stretch stays on fire are assumed in the bullets above. And the ratios come from 512-byte value cherries of a 2B-parameter LLM, carried to the 640-byte MLA latents of GLM-5.1 (III.4 Two wrinkles: RoPE and MLA).

The pick traces, measured on 2026-11-03 (XI.1 The result on one page). Exact top-k attention of Qwen 2.5 1.5B over five coding-agent sequences stood in for the indexer, and HiSparse’s hot cache and a dictionary shelf were replayed on its picks. At the Pin’s share of the context the stand-in misses 35 / 19 / 9.7 % with 1, 2 and 4 slots per pick, against HiSparse’s 30 / 13.4 / 6.7 %. Misses pile into stretches above chance (a shelf catches 3–5× what an even spread gives), but spread as the context grows: at the Pin’s 100K, extrapolated, four dictionaries of 4096 positions catch 36–54 % of the misses, not 0.85. With every design at its best hot-cache size, HiSparse resized to about 4740 slots serves 127 sequences per GPU, light dictionaries on a shelf 127–133, tree dictionaries on a shelf 88–108, and a solo code on every miss, with no bytes per sequence on the GPU, 141, if a GPU decodes it at 500 GB/s or more (XI.10 The slot price). Longer stretches make every dictionary shelf worse, because a dictionary on the GPU is resident memory (XI.9 The other half of the bill). And by the half-gain rule no code of the miss path can add more than about +31 % at the Pin: 166 sequences per GPU at the extrapolated floor of 0.57 (XI.11 The ceiling of the miss path).

So the project pays only if the memory lever is built, and only in the band where the heavy dictionary replaces the slots it duplicates. The light compressor is worth it if it comes almost for free. Both statements rested on assumed traffic; the replay above replaced it with measured counts, and with them the best design measured keeps no dictionary per sequence.

Takeaway
  • Lever 1 alone: about 1.2× at best, before the fixed-length penalty. Lever 2 with the tree compressor: about 1.1× on the Pin, more where GPU memory is cheap relative to link bytes.
  • Assumed, not measured: the δ-miss share, the load rate, the fixed-length rate, the transfer to GLM-5.1.

The plan

In order of how fast each returns a result, using the data that exists (X.1 Where we stand, X.2 Principles: speed first).

  1. More scenarios in the harness (X.4 Next in the harness): keys under a leash; K+V cherries; stereo across layers; sequence-scope dictionaries; the leash at 137 against 293; the bit-field split as a stage; prose text; a GPU-speed test of the frontier compressor.
  2. Queries and a stand-in indexer (X.5 Queries and picks: a stand-in indexer): compute q for the Bench’s dump in one pass of the LLM, run exact top-k attention as the indexer, and record a pick trace per sequence. This is what the Bench lacks (X.3 The dataset we have, and what to add cheaply).
  3. Replay the hot cache (X.6 Replaying the hot cache): miss rates, where misses land, stretch popularity, the on-fire simulation, fD​ and nload​ measured instead of assumed, with shuffled traces as the control.
  4. How fast must the decoder be (X.7 How fast must the decoder be?): the 10× rule and a GPU decode of the frontier compressor; then a sparse-attention LLM with a real indexer and a victim-tier replay (X.8 Beyond the harness).

The readings and the next step for each outcome are written before the data (X.9 What each outcome would mean); the weak spots are listed (X.10 Weak spots, risks and pivots).

Next action

Compute the queries for the dump, run the stand-in indexer, replay the hot cache with the measured 0.686 and 0.636 points, and read fD​, nload​ and the lifetime of a stretch on fire. Those three numbers turn the napkin above into a measurement.
