IV.14

The trap: the exchange rate is harsh

Suppose the cat-thread ladder is real and every rung explains a healthy share of an entry. How many bits does that buy? Run the numbers through Part II’s exchange rate and the answer is sobering — with one escape hatch.

IV.12 What the ladder gets right granted the ladder its direction: each narrower crowd can only make an entry cheaper. Now grant it more — suppose each rung explains a solid share of what an entry looks like. The question becomes arithmetic: how many bits does a solid share buy?

The ladder as a variance budget

Think of one element of an entry as a number that varies from token to token. Split its variance into four parts:

  • a part explained by what all texts share (a global dictionary);
  • a further part explained by the conversation (a per-thread dictionary);
  • a further part explained by the neighbourhood (a per-block dictionary);
  • and the rest — token identity, syntax, position, rounding — which no dictionary explains: noise.

Then price each rung with the exchange rate of Part II.

ReminderThe exchange rate

For bell-shaped data, a predictor that explains a fraction R2 of a value’s variance leaves a residual of variance (1−R2)σ2 and saves 21​log2​1−R21​ bits per value: 75 % explained → 1 bit, 94 % → 2 bits (II.12 The exchange rate: variance explained → bits).

With the defaults — 30 % global, 20 % thread, 20 % block, 30 % noise — the global rung saves 0.26 bit per element, the thread rung another 0.24, and the block rung, the one kv-blockdiff is built on, 0.37. The whole ladder: 0.87 bit out of 8.

☞ Things to try
  1. Try to get one full bit from the block rung alone. The table’s note tells you what it takes: the block must explain three quarters of whatever the thread left.
  2. Push all three rungs up until noise is 10 %. Even then the whole ladder saves about 1.7 bits.
  3. Watch the shaded noise region: whatever you do with the rungs, the total cannot pass 21​log2​noise1​.

Why is the rate so harsh? Because “about the same subject” is a small fraction of an entry. The subject of a paragraph — Maine Coon or Python — plausibly lives in a few directions of a 640-element entry. The other elements carry which token this is, its role in the sentence, its position, and plain rounding noise. A lossless code pays for every one of them.

Running themeLossless pays for all the noise

The noise share is a hard floor. With 30 % of the variance unexplainable, no dictionary of any scope can save more than 0.87 bit per element — the shaded region of the plot is out of reach for every rung.

Takeaway
  • Price the ladder as a variance budget: each rung saves 21​log2​ of the ratio of residual variances.
  • Generous defaults (30/20/20, 30 % noise) give the block rung 0.37 bit and the whole ladder 0.87 bit per element.
  • One bit from the block rung alone needs it to explain ¾ of what the thread left.

The escape hatch: concentrated structure

There is one way out, and it comes from the shape of the exchange rate itself. Bits per element are an average over channels. The exchange rate is gentle for modest R2 and steep near R2=1: a channel whose variance is 98 % explainable saves 21​log2​50≈2.8 bits, more than three times what a typical channel saves under the default budget.

So the same total predictability, concentrated in a few channels, buys more bits than when it is spread evenly. The compare block below makes that exact: 70 % of the variance explained on average either way, 0.87 bit spread evenly, 1.0 bit concentrated. In the calculator’s lower part you can also add such channels to the default budget: make 10 % of the channels 98 % explainable and the average rises from 0.87 to about 1.06 bits per element — a tenth of the channels adds half as much as the whole block rung.

Do KV caches have such channels? There are hints. Papers on KV quantization report that keys have a few channels whose values are much larger than the rest and change slowly from token to token (V.7 KIVI (2024), V.8 KVQuant (2024)). If those channels are near-deterministic at block scale, they are the escape hatch. But three things are unknown: whether they survive at 8-bit precision, how many of the entry’s bytes they own, and how the 8-bit number grid treats a prediction (VI.11 The 8-bit grid gives and takes shows that a logarithmic grid can eat much of the gain).

Two ways to explain 70 % of the variance, on average
Spread evenly
  • Every channel: 70 % explained, 30 % noise.
  • Every channel saves 0.87 bit.
  • Average: 0.87 bit per element.
Concentrated (toy)
  • 10 % of channels: 98 % explained (2.8 bits each).
  • The other 90 %: 67 % explained (0.80 bit each).
  • Average: 1.0 bit per element.

That is the honest position. The napkin does not offer a shortcut around measurement; it is the reason measurement is needed. Wave 1 measures, per layer, for keys and values separately, how much of an entry is near-deterministic at each scope (IX.7 Experiment 1b: the exact lossless ladder).

Takeaway
  • Bits are averaged over channels, and the exchange rate is steep near R2=1: a few near-deterministic channels can carry the gain.
  • Keys reportedly have such channels; whether they survive at 8 bits, and how much they own, is unmeasured.
  • The trap is not a refutation — it sets the expected size, and the size must be measured.
